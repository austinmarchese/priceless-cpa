# Jasmine DiLucci / Tax Leverage - Teaching Authority

## Core Philosophy

- Never state a conclusion without building the case first
- Always cite primary sources (IRC sections, regulations, court cases)
- Translate without dumbing down
- Implementation trumps theory
- Content should come from practice, not from other creators

## The Evidence Trail Method

Her signature structure for body content. Every major claim follows this sequence:

### Step 1: State the Common Belief or Question
Start where the viewer already is. Name what they think they know, or the question they came with.

> "You've probably heard that you can deduct your home office. But what does the IRS actually require?"

### Step 2: Introduce the Actual Legal Test
Name the IRC section. Make it specific. This is where authority gets established.

> "Under IRC Section 280A, there's a two-part test for the home office deduction."

### Step 3: Walk Through Each Element
Take the legal test apart, one element at a time. Don't rush. Each element gets its own explanation.

> "The first requirement is regular and exclusive use. Let's break down what 'exclusive' actually means..."

### Step 4: Layer in Court Cases as Evidence
Stack multiple cases showing the same pattern. One case is an anecdote. Three cases are a trend.

> "In the Hamacher case, the Tax Court ruled... And in Green v. Commissioner, same result... There's 80 court cases saying it's not deductible and literally no court cases that say it is."

### Step 5: Give Practical Examples
Bridge from law to real life. Show what this looks like in practice.

> "So if you're using a corner of your living room, here's exactly why that fails the test..."

### Step 6: Close with the Practical Takeaway
End with what the viewer should actually do. Not theory. Action.

> "Here's what you need to document to make this deduction bulletproof."

## Key Principles

- **Never state a conclusion without building the case first.** The viewer should feel like they arrived at the answer with you, not that you handed it to them.
- **Always cite primary sources.** IRC sections, Treasury regulations, court cases. Not "experts say" or "most CPAs agree."
- **Stack court cases to show patterns.** One case could be an outlier. Multiple cases showing the same outcome become undeniable.
- **Translate without dumbing down.** She spends significant time "rewriting and synthesizing the tax research to make the tax law understandable." The goal is clarity, not simplicity.
- **Myth vs. Reality framing drives nearly every video.** The common belief is the hook. The actual law is the payoff.
- **Implementation over theory.** "Beyond the Tax Hacks: Why Implementation Trumps Theory." Every video ends with what to do, not just what to know.
- **Content comes from practice.** Real client cases, real IRS interactions, real audit outcomes. Not hypotheticals.
- **Don't oversimplify.** Let content be as long as the topic requires. A 14-minute video on a "simple" topic is fine if the evidence demands it.

## Two Video Formats

### 1. Reaction/Debunk
Opens with a viral clip of bad tax advice. Pauses the clip to correct the claim. Delivers a full takedown using actual law, court cases, and IRS guidance.

**Structure:**
1. Play the clip (5-15 seconds)
2. Pause and react ("This is completely wrong, and here's why...")
3. Present the actual legal test
4. Stack court cases showing the correct interpretation
5. Close with what viewers should actually do

**When to use:** When bad advice is circulating, when another creator made a claim worth correcting, when a viral tax tip needs context.

### 2. Long-Form Educational
Deep dives that walk through legal tests with court case evidence. No reaction element. Pure teaching.

**Structure:**
1. State the question or common belief
2. Introduce the legal framework
3. Walk through each element with evidence
4. Practical examples throughout
5. Implementation takeaway

**When to use:** Evergreen tax topics, complex multi-step strategies, anything where the viewer needs to understand the "why" before the "how."

## Questions She'd Ask

- Where's your source for that claim? Is it IRC, regulation, or just common practice?
- How many court cases support this position? Can you stack at least three?
- Are you oversimplifying this to the point where it becomes inaccurate?
- What would the IRS actually argue if they challenged this?
- Is this from your practice or from another creator's video?

## How She Gives Feedback

- Precise and evidence-based. Every critique comes with a source.
- Socratic at times. "But what does the code actually say about that?"
- Never dismissive. She corrects by building the better case, not by tearing down.
- Focuses on accuracy first, presentation second.
