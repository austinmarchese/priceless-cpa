# California (CA) — State Tax File

**The most complex state in the union and the most consequential for Priceless tax planning.** California is the most common ORIGIN state for Priceless clients relocating to Florida, and Priceless has direct CA clients with multi-state complexities. Every Priceless engagement involving CA requires careful state-federal coordination because California aggressively decouples from federal tax law.

## Quick reference

- **Top marginal rate**: 13.3% (highest in the country) — 12.3% top bracket + 1% Mental Health Services Tax (MHST) on income > $1M
- **9-bracket progressive system**: 1% to 12.3% + MHST
- **No local income tax** (FTB administers state-level only)
- **PTET available** under AB 150 — elective 9.3% entity-level tax with owner credit; SALT cap workaround
- **LLC annual fee + minimum tax**: $800 minimum tax + tiered gross receipts fee up to $11,790
- **S Corp tax**: 1.5% of net income + $800 minimum
- **Corporate income tax**: 8.84% C corp / 10.84% banks
- **Sales tax**: 7.25% state base + local up to 3.5% (max ~10.75% in some areas)
- **Property tax**: 1% under Prop 13, capped at 2% annual assessment increases
- **Estate tax**: NONE at state level (federal only)
- **OBBBA non-conformity**: CRITICAL — California explicitly does NOT incorporate OBBBA changes
- **Capital gains**: taxed as ordinary income at top 13.3%
- **No QSBS exclusion** at state level (CA does not conform to §1202)
- **No bonus depreciation** at state level (CA decouples from §168(k))
- **§179 limit**: $25,000 (vs. federal $2.5M post-OBBBA)

For Priceless purposes: California requires SEPARATE state and federal calculations on virtually every strategy. Federal benefits often have NO California counterpart, making the effective tax savings of federal strategies smaller for CA residents. This is the primary reason CA residents benefit so much from FL/TX relocations (eliminate the 13.3% layer entirely).

## The CRITICAL non-conformity issue (read first)

**California operates a distinct income tax system that does NOT automatically follow federal tax law.**

California's general IRC conformity date moved to January 1, 2025 in late 2025, but this update **EXCLUDES** the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA). California also continues its longstanding practice of selectively decoupling from many federal provisions.

This means for every federal strategy, you must run a separate California analysis. The rest of this file enumerates the specific non-conformities with their dollar impacts.

California allows itemized deductions but with significant differences from federal:
- **State and local taxes (SALT)**: California allows full SALT deduction at state level (no $40,400 cap; that's federal only)

- **Charitable contributions**: California decouples from OBBBA's 0.5% AGI floor (CA does not have the floor)
- **Miscellaneous deductions**: California still allows certain miscellaneous deductions that TCJA eliminated federally (state itemized deduction worksheet)

For Priceless clients in CA: state itemized deduction is often substantially higher than federal itemized after the SALT cap.

## Mental Health Services Tax (Proposition 63 / §17043)

Additional 1% tax on individuals with taxable income exceeding $1,000,000:
- Applies to ALL income above $1M, not graduated
- Combined with 12.3% top bracket = 13.3% effective top marginal rate
- For Priceless Full-Wealth clients, this is essentially a guaranteed addition

For an individual with $2M California taxable income:
- Tax up to $1M: standard bracket buildup
- Tax on income $1M-$2M: 13.3% (12.3% + 1% MHST)
- Marginal rate matters dramatically for any timing strategy

## Capital gains taxation

**California taxes capital gains as ORDINARY INCOME.**

This is one of the most significant CA disadvantages:
- Federal: long-term capital gains at 0/15/20% + 3.8% NIIT (max 23.8%)
- California: capital gains taxed at standard income brackets up to 13.3%
- No California preferential rate
- Combined federal + state for top earners: 23.8% + 13.3% = ~37.1%

For any client considering large gain realization:
- §1031 exchange: defers both federal AND California
- QOZ: defers federal; California taxes appreciation
- QSBS: federal exclusion; California has NO exclusion (non-conformity)
- Installment sale: spreads gain at California rate over years
- Move to FL/TX before sale: eliminates California layer

This is why pre-sale residency planning is so valuable for CA clients with imminent liquidity events.

## OBBBA conformity analysis (critical for every CA engagement)

| OBBBA Provision | Federal Treatment | California Treatment | Practical Impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100% bonus depreciation (post-1/19/2025) | Permanent | DOES NOT CONFORM (long-standing decoupling) | CA depreciation continues over MACRS lives; no state benefit; significant federal/state difference; separate depreciation schedules required |
| §199A QBI deduction permanent | Permanent at 20% | DOES NOT CONFORM | CA has NO QBI deduction; pass-through owners pay full CA tax on K-1 income |
| §163(j) interest limitation back to EBITDA | EBITDA basis | DOES NOT CONFORM | CA continues pre-OBBBA EBIT basis (more restrictive); may limit interest deduction |
| §174 R&D domestic immediate expensing | Restored permanent | CA conforms to §174 as it read on Jan 1, 2015 | CA still requires capitalization and amortization of R&D over 5 years (or 15 for foreign); major divergence |
| §1202 QSBS expanded ($15M cap, tiered) | $15M cap, 3yr/50%, 4yr/75%, 5yr/100% | NO QSBS EXCLUSION at state level | California taxes ALL QSBS gain at ordinary rates (up to 13.3%); biggest single non-conformity for tech sector |
| QOZ permanent + Dec 31 2031 trigger; new program 2027 | Permanent with new mechanics | CA decouples on key features | CA generally does NOT exempt QOF appreciation; CA taxes deferred gain when triggered + appreciation; significantly reduces QOZ benefit for CA residents |
| §250 GILTI deduction | Continued | Generally CA does NOT conform | C corps with CFC income face full CA tax on GILTI; no §250 deduction relief |
| §179 expensing $2.5M / $4M phase-out | $2.5M / $4M phase-out 2026 | CA limit $25,000 (decouples from increases) | Massive divergence; CA limit is 1% of federal limit |
| Estate exemption $15M permanent | $15M | N/A (CA has no state estate tax) | Federal-only estate tax for CA residents |
| SALT cap $40,400 (2026) | $40,400 with phase-down | CA has no SALT cap at state level (allows full SALT at state) | PTET workaround targets federal cap; CA-level deduction unchanged |
| Charitable 0.5% AGI floor (2026+) | 0.5% floor | CA decouples; no floor | CA charitable deduction continues without floor; may differ materially from federal |
| 35% bracket cap on charitable deduction | 35% cap | CA decouples | CA charitable continues at full bracket value (up to 13.3%) |

### What this means in practice

For a CA Priceless client doing $500K cost segregation study:
- Federal: 100% bonus on $500K eligible property = $500K year-one deduction → $185K saved at 37%
- California: CA depreciates $500K over 5-7 years straight line (or MACRS without bonus) → first-year CA deduction approximately $100K → CA tax saved $13.3K
- Federal-state difference on year one: $400K of "phantom" CA income vs. federal
- Multi-year reconciliation: CA depreciation eventually matches federal cumulative; timing difference

This timing difference creates two complications:
1. CA owes more current-year tax than the federal-only analysis suggests
2. Need to maintain SEPARATE federal and state depreciation schedules indefinitely
3. CA basis in disposed property differs from federal; gain on sale calculated separately

### The QSBS divergence (most consequential for tech clients)

Software/AI/tech clients exiting QSBS-eligible companies face dramatically different federal vs. California outcomes:

For $30M QSBS sale (basis $300K):
- Federal: $15M excluded (post-OBBBA cap) + $15M taxed at 28% = $4.2M federal
- California: $0 excluded + $30M × 13.3% = $3.99M California
- Combined: $8.19M (vs. potential $0 if held by FL resident)

A FL resident with same QSBS exit: $4.2M federal + $0 state = $4.2M total
A CA resident: $8.19M total
Differential: ~$3.99M California cost of being a CA resident

This is the fundamental driver of pre-IPO/pre-exit residency planning conversations for CA tech clients. Move to FL or other no-tax state at least 6+ months before exit (longer is better) and survive CA residency audit = save the CA portion.

## PTET — Pass-Through Entity Elective Tax (AB 150)

California's SALT cap workaround. Critical strategy for CA pass-through entity owners.

### Mechanics

Under AB 150 (Cal. Rev. & Tax. Code §17052.10):
- Qualifying pass-through entities (S corps, partnerships, LLCs taxed as partnerships) can elect annually to pay 9.3% California tax at the ENTITY level on qualified income
- Entity claims federal deduction for tax paid (sidestepping SALT cap)
- Owners receive credit on their CA personal return for tax paid by entity
- Net effect: SALT cap circumvented; federal benefit captured

### Election mechanics

- **Election form**: FTB Form 3804
- **Election timing**: must elect annually
- **Payment requirements**: Form 3893
- **Payment deadlines**:
  - First payment due by June 15 of taxable year (50% of expected PTET liability OR $1,000)
  - Final payment due by original return due date (March 15 / April 15)
- **Credit on personal return**: Form 540 with credit attached

### Who qualifies for the election

- Partnerships
- LLCs taxed as partnerships
- S corporations
- Single-member LLC's owners do NOT qualify (the SMLLC is a disregarded entity for this purpose)

### Who is NOT eligible

- Sole proprietors (Schedule C)
- Single-member LLCs (disregarded)
- Publicly traded partnerships
- Corporations with members that are not individuals/estates/trusts/other PTET-electing entities (limitations)

### The math (PTET advantage)

For CA S Corp owner with $500K K-1 income (top bracket):

WITHOUT PTET:
- $500K K-1 income
- CA tax owed by owner: $500K × 9.3% = $46,500
- Federal: limited by $40,400 SALT cap; partial deduction
- Net federal benefit on CA tax: limited

WITH PTET:
- S Corp pays $46,500 to CA via PTET (entity-level)
- S Corp deducts $46,500 federally → reduces K-1 by that amount
- Owner's K-1 reduced from $500K to $453,500
- Federal tax savings on $46,500 deduction: $46,500 × 37% = $17,205
- Owner gets $46,500 CA credit on personal return → no additional CA tax owed at owner level
- Net benefit: ~$17,000 federal savings

### Ongoing PTET status

- AB 150 originally scheduled to sunset December 31, 2025
- **SB 132 enacted June 27, 2025 extended PTET through December 31, 2030** — five-year extension with modifications
- **New 12.5% credit-reduction mechanic for 2026-2030**: Missing or underpaying the June 15 prepayment no longer invalidates the election (as it did for 2022-2025), but each shortfall dollar reduces the owner's PTET credit by 12.5%. This provides operational flexibility for entities with unpredictable income while maintaining incentive for timely payment.
- **Payment rules for 2026-2030 tax years**:
  - First payment: on or before June 15 of taxable year — greater of $1,000 or 50% of prior year PTET (missing this only reduces credit now; does not kill the election)
  - Second payment: on or before the original return due date (March 15 of following year)
- Federal SALT cap continues post-OBBBA at $40,400 (2026) with phase-down above $505K MAGI to $10K floor — PTET continues to provide value as long as federal SALT cap exists, which is permanent under OBBBA (cap drops to $10,000 permanently starting 2030 per the current law)

### PTET decision factors

PTET makes sense when:
- Owner is in 9.3% or higher bracket
- SALT cap is materially constraining federal deduction
- Pass-through entity has clear separation from owner
- Estimated CA tax > $1,000 threshold

PTET may not make sense when:
- Owner has low CA income (lower bracket)
- Owner has significant other CA tax not generating PTET-eligible income
- Cash flow timing issues (PTET requires entity-level payments)

### Multi-state implications

For multi-state pass-through entities:
- PTET applies to CA-source income only
- Coordinate with PTET in other states (NY, NJ, etc.)
- Apportionment determines how much income subject to CA PTET
- See `tax-strategy/strategies/PTET-ELECTION-BY-STATE.md` for multi-state PTET coordination

## Corporate income tax

### C corporations (Form 100)

- **Rate**: 8.84% on net income
- **Minimum tax**: $800 (if profitable)
- **Banks and financial corporations**: 10.84%
- **Apportionment**: Single sales factor (as of 2013) for most businesses; some industries continue three-factor

### S corporations (Form 100S)

California S corps face additional state-level tax (one of few states):
- **Net income tax**: 1.5% of net income
- **Minimum tax**: $800 annually (even at zero income)
- **Federal pass-through preserved** with state-level entity tax

This means CA S corp shareholders effectively pay BOTH the entity-level 1.5% plus their personal income tax on the K-1 distribution. PTET election (above) reduces this by replacing the personal-level CA tax.

### LLC fees and minimum tax

CA imposes both a flat $800 minimum tax AND a tiered gross receipts fee on LLCs (Cal. Rev. & Tax. Code §17935, §17942):

| Total Income (Gross Receipts) | LLC Fee |
|---|---|
| $0 - $249,999 | $0 |
| $250,000 - $499,999 | $900 |
| $500,000 - $999,999 | $2,500 |
| $1,000,000 - $4,999,999 | $6,000 |
| $5,000,000+ | $11,790 |

The LLC fee is on top of the $800 minimum tax. So a CA LLC with $2M gross receipts owes $800 + $6,000 = $6,800 in entity-level CA fees BEFORE considering net income tax pass-through to members.

This is why many CA-active businesses structure as S corps rather than LLCs (avoid the LLC fee; S corps pay 1.5% on net income but no gross receipts fee).

### Apportionment

California uses single-sales-factor apportionment for most multi-state corporations:
- Sales factor = CA sales / total sales
- All income apportioned by sales factor
- Cost of performance vs. market-based sourcing for services (varies by industry)
- Throwback rule: CA-origin sales to no-nexus states "thrown back" to CA

For multi-state Priceless clients with CA operations, single-sales factor is friendly to in-state employers / multi-state sellers and unfriendly to in-state sellers / multi-state employers.

## Sales and use tax

### Base rate

- **State rate**: 7.25% (highest base rate in the nation)
- **Mandatory local additions**: included in 7.25%
- **District taxes (additional)**: 0.10% to 1% (local discretion)
- **Combined statewide range**: 7.25% to 10.75% (LA County areas)
- **Average combined rate**: ~8.85%

### What's taxed

Generally:
- Tangible personal property (most goods)
- Some services (limited compared to most states)
- NOT food for home consumption
- NOT prescription drugs
- NOT custom software (typically) but pre-written software taxable

### SaaS treatment

California generally does NOT impose sales tax on cloud/SaaS where the customer doesn't take physical possession. Services delivered electronically are typically not taxable (with exceptions). Verify per specific facts.

### Wayfair / nexus

Economic nexus threshold for remote sellers:
- $500,000 in annual sales of tangible personal property delivered into California
- Marketplace facilitator rules apply

### Use tax

CA use tax applies to purchases from out-of-state vendors not collecting CA sales tax. Reported on personal return (Line 91 of Form 540) for individuals.

## Property tax (Proposition 13 framework)

California property tax operates under Proposition 13 (1978) and subsequent measures:

### Base assessment (Prop 13)

- **Base year value**: market value at time of acquisition
- **Annual increase cap**: 2% maximum (or CPI, whichever lower)
- **Rate**: 1% of assessed value (statewide cap)
- **Plus**: voter-approved bond and special assessments (typically add 0.1%-0.5%)

### Reassessment events

Property reassessed to current market value upon:
- Change in ownership (sale, gift)
- New construction
- Certain entity ownership changes (LLCs, partnerships)

### Proposition 19 (2020 — major change)

Limited parent-child reassessment exclusion:
- Pre-Prop 19: parent-child transfer of any property received Prop 13 base year transfer
- Post-Prop 19 (effective Feb 16, 2021): parent-child transfer ONLY excluded for primary residence + only if child uses as primary residence
- Investment properties, vacation homes, and second residences NOW REASSESS upon transfer

Major impact for CA real estate-owning families. Estate planning that previously relied on parent-child Prop 13 transfer for investment properties no longer works.

For Priceless CA clients with appreciated investment real estate:
- Inherited properties WILL reassess to market value
- Gift during life triggers same reassessment
- Pre-Prop 19 transfers grandfathered (already done before Feb 16, 2021)

This is a major estate planning consideration that often requires coordination with CA estate attorney.

### Prop 19 portability for primary residence

Senior citizens (55+) and disabled persons can transfer Prop 13 base year value to new primary residence anywhere in California (3-time use limit). Useful for downsizing without losing low assessed value.

## Residency rules (extensive)

California aggressively asserts residency for income tax purposes. This is one of the most-audited areas for high-income individuals.

### California residency definition (Rev. & Tax. Code §17014)

A "resident" is:
- **Domiciliary**: every individual who is in California for other than a temporary or transitory purpose
- **9-month presumption**: any individual who is in California for an aggregate of more than 9 months during the taxable year is PRESUMED to be a resident (rebuttable)

Note: California uses 9-month rule, NOT 183-day rule. This is unusual and stricter than most states.

### Domicile vs. residency

California distinguishes:
- **Domicile**: legal "true, fixed home" — only one at a time
- **Residency**: where person lives at given time; can be in CA without being domiciled there
- **Both can result in CA tax obligations** depending on facts

### The 9-month rule

If individual physically present in California for more than 9 months total in tax year:
- Presumed CA resident
- Tax CA on worldwide income
- Burden of proof on taxpayer to rebut

This is more aggressive than 183-day rule used by NY, NJ, etc.

### Nine non-residency factors (FTB Pub 1031)

California considers many factors in residency determinations:
1. Amount of time spent in CA vs. elsewhere
2. Location of spouse and minor children
3. Location of principal residence
4. Where personal items kept
5. Location of vehicles and registration state
6. Source of compensation
7. Location of place of employment
8. Location of bank accounts
9. Location of medical care providers, lawyers, CPAs
10. Voter registration
11. Driver's license state
12. Membership in clubs and organizations
13. Telephone listing
14. Mailing address for important documents

No single factor controls; FTB looks at totality of circumstances.

### "Safe harbor" under §17014(d)

Taxpayer who would otherwise be CA resident may be treated as non-resident if:
- Absent from CA for an "uninterrupted period of at least 546 consecutive days"
- Pursuant to an employment-related contract
- During the period of absence: in CA no more than 45 days per tax year; spouse in CA no more than 45 days per tax year
- Intangible income from CA-source not a primary income source

This is the "546-day rule" — used by some high-income individuals working abroad.

### Tax on residents (worldwide income)

CA residents pay California tax on:
- All income from all sources, regardless of where earned
- Worldwide income basis

### Tax on non-residents (CA-source only)

Non-residents pay CA tax on:
- CA-source income only
- Income from CA real estate
- Income from business operations in CA
- Wages for services performed in CA
- CA-source pass-through income
- Sale of CA real estate

### Part-year residents

Part-year residents (moved into or out of CA during year) file Form 540NR:
- CA tax on worldwide income for resident period
- CA tax on CA-source income for non-resident period
- Apportionment between periods

### CA aggressive audit posture

FTB residency audit team is among the most aggressive in the country:
- Systematic review of high-income exits
- Examines bank/credit card geolocation data
- Subpoenas cell phone records
- Reviews social media and travel records
- Looks back 4+ years (longer if no return filed)
- Uses Bonta v. FTB and other case law to assert continued residency for departing taxpayers

For Priceless clients leaving CA:
- Document the move EXTENSIVELY
- Sell CA primary residence (or convert to clearly-investment status with rental)
- Move spouse and dependents
- Establish overt acts in new state (covered in `STATE-FL.md` for FL relocations)
- Maintain detailed day-counting records
- Engage CA residency specialist for the transition year
- Expect audit; build the file from day one

### Common Priceless residency scenarios for CA clients

**Scenario A: Tech executive moving to FL pre-IPO exit**
- Critical: establish FL residency BEFORE liquidity event
- Minimum 18+ months in new state preferred
- Sell CA home or convert to rental
- Document everything
- File CA part-year return for transition year
- Following year: CA non-resident return for any continuing CA-source income

**Scenario B: Real estate investor with CA properties moving to NV**
- CA-source income (rental from CA properties) continues to be CA-taxable
- Sale of CA properties: CA tax on gain regardless of residency
- §1031 exchange to non-CA property: defers but doesn't eliminate
- Estate considerations: §1014 step-up at death potentially eliminates CA gain (for federal; CA conforms)

**Scenario C: Snowbird splitting time between CA and FL**
- Stay under 9 months in CA to avoid presumption
- Document the FL ties (DL, voter reg, etc.)
- Maintain primary domicile in FL
- Watch for "principal place of abode" tests
- Risk: CA may still claim residency if CA presence regular and substantial

## Industry-specific notes (CA-focused)

### Real estate (high CA exposure)

- Prop 13 base year value protections valuable for long-held properties
- Prop 19 (2021) eliminated parent-child investment property exclusion
- §1031 exchange: CA conforms; both federal and CA defer
- Cost segregation: CA does NOT conform to federal bonus depreciation; state benefit much smaller
- CA capital gains taxed at ordinary rates (up to 13.3%)
- Documentary transfer tax: $1.10 per $1,000 county + city varies
- Mello-Roos special assessments common in newer developments

### Software / AI / SaaS (huge CA disadvantage)

- No QSBS exclusion at state level (entire $15M federal exclusion lost for CA tax)
- No QBI deduction
- R&D capitalization continues at state level (federal §174A doesn't apply for CA)
- Stock option exercise: CA-source income for services performed in CA
- This is fundamental driver of tech relocations FROM CA

### Doctors / medical

- All income taxed at top brackets for high earners
- Practice income subject to CA personal income tax
- LLC fee + minimum tax for practice entities
- Solo 401(k) and DB plan contributions deductible at state level (CA conforms here)

### E-commerce

- Sales tax compliance complex (large CA sales = nexus)
- District taxes vary by ship-to address
- LLC fee on gross receipts for CA-formed entities

### Investment firms / RIAs

- CA-source management fees taxable at top brackets
- §475 mark-to-market (traders): CA conforms
- Investment income from CA sources to non-residents: typically not CA-taxed (intangibles sourced to residence state)

## Multi-state considerations

### CA has no PTET coordination requirement with other states

For multi-state pass-through clients, CA PTET applies only to CA-source income. Other states' PTETs apply to their respective sourcing.

### Common multi-state PTET scenarios

- CA + NY pass-through: separate PTET elections in each state
- CA + FL pass-through: PTET in CA only (FL has no PTET)
- CA + multi-state apportionment: PTET applies to apportioned CA share

### Apportionment specifically

- CA single sales factor (most industries)
- Throwback rule (CA-origin sales to no-nexus states stay CA)
- Combined unitary reporting required for affiliates with substantial CA business

## Common Priceless engagement scenarios

### Scenario A: CA tech employee with QSBS exit

Client: Senior engineer at private tech company, exit imminent (acquisition $50M valuation; client owns $5M QSBS).

CA stay analysis:

### Scenario B: CA S Corp owner $750K K-1 considering PTET

### Scenario C: CA real estate investor with cost seg

### Scenario D: CA Full-Wealth client considering QOZ

Client: $2M capital gain from business sale; considering QOZ deferral.

Federal QOZ benefit:
- Defer $2M × 23.8% = $476K federal until 2031 (post-OBBBA trigger)
- Hold 10 years: appreciation excluded federally

California QOZ analysis:
- CA does NOT conform to federal QOZ deferral mechanics (mostly)
- CA generally taxes the gain when realized federally
- CA generally taxes QOF appreciation when realized at year 10
- CA QOZ benefit: minimal compared to federal

For CA client with $2M gain:
- Federal benefit from QOZ: substantial
- CA cost: $2M × 13.3% = $266K CA tax owed regardless of QOZ structure
- Net benefit: federal savings minus CA tax of ~$266K already due

Compared with FL resident: same federal benefit + zero CA cost.

This is why pre-exit residency change is often more valuable than QOZ deferral for CA residents.

## Audit posture (CA — among most aggressive)

CA Franchise Tax Board (FTB) is one of the most aggressive state tax authorities in the country:

- Specific residency audit team for high-income exits
- Subpoenas for cell phone records, credit card statements
- Cross-references federal data
- Deep look-back periods (4+ years standard; longer for non-filers)
- Aggressive interpretation of "temporary or transitory" purpose
- Pursues collection across state lines

Common audit triggers:
- High-income exit from CA (residency challenge)
- Dramatic income drop in CA
- Sale of large CA business
- QSBS exclusion claimed by ex-CA resident
- Pass-through K-1 income reduction post-move
- Failure to file CA non-resident return when CA-source income present

Statute of limitations:
- General: 4 years from return due date (longer than federal 3-year)
- No statute if no return filed
- Fraud: no statute
- 6-year for substantial understatement
