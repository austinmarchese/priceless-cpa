# Hiring Children Legitimately

A widely-marketed strategy that produces real value when done right. Most failures come from treating it as a paper exercise rather than legitimate employment.

## The basic mechanic

Parent business pays child for work performed. Child's wages:
- Deductible to parent's business as ordinary §162 expense
- Taxable income to child (but standard deduction covers wages up to $16,100 in 2026 / $15,750 in 2025; federal income tax on wages within that = $0)
- Exempt from FICA/Medicare if employer is sole prop, single-member LLC of parent, or partnership where all partners are parents (per §3121(b)(3))
- Exempt from FUTA if child is under 21 (§3306(c)(5))
- Subject to state income tax depending on state

Net effect at federal level: parent transfers up to $16,100/year (2026) of income from parent's marginal bracket (often 32-37%) to child's 0% bracket = $5,150-$5,960 federal tax savings per child per year at parent's top bracket.

## Critical: the FICA exemption only applies to certain employers

| Employer type | FICA on child's wages |
|---|---|
| Sole proprietorship of parent | EXEMPT (under 18) |
| Single-member LLC of parent (default) | EXEMPT (under 18) |
| Partnership where ALL partners are parents | EXEMPT (under 18) |
| Partnership with any non-parent partner | NOT EXEMPT |
| S Corp | NOT EXEMPT — pays FICA |
| C Corp | NOT EXEMPT — pays FICA |
| Single-member LLC taxed as S Corp | NOT EXEMPT |

For S Corp owners, the standard workaround: have the **parent S Corp owner** pay child personally as household employee (no FICA exemption) OR establish a separate sole prop / family management company that contracts with the S Corp.

The "family management company" structure: parent operates sole prop "Family Management Co" that provides services to the S Corp; sole prop hires children, paying their wages with FICA exemption; sole prop bills S Corp for services.

This works but is a complex structure and IRS sometimes scrutinizes the legitimacy of the management company.

## What "legitimate work" means

The single biggest audit issue. Work must be:
- Real (actually performed)
- Age-appropriate (5-year-olds cannot legitimately do bookkeeping; 16-year-olds can)
- Beneficial to the business
- Performed (not just claimed)
- Compensated at reasonable rate for the work

## Age-appropriate work examples

**Ages 7-10**: Light tasks like model for marketing photos, simple sorting/packaging, cleaning under direct supervision, simple data entry of names/addresses

**Ages 11-13**: Filing, basic admin, social media post creation, product testing for online business, basic inventory counting

**Ages 14-15**: Standard part-time work (with state child labor law compliance), more complex admin, customer service, video editing, content creation

**Ages 16-17**: Substantively similar work to adult part-time employee

**Under 7**: Very limited legitimate work; documentation must be extra thorough; consider whether the strategy is worth the audit risk

## Reasonable wage rate

Wage must be commensurate with the work. A 12-year-old doing 5 hours/week of social media posts can be paid $15-25/hour realistically. Same child cannot be paid $200/hour for the same work — IRS reclassifies as gift or distribution.

Rule of thumb: pay what you'd pay an unrelated person of comparable age and capability for the same work.

## Implementation steps

1. Identify legitimate work the business needs done that child can perform
2. Document job description with age-appropriate tasks
3. Set hourly rate consistent with work value
4. Set up payroll (or for very small amounts, document via 1099-NEC if treating as independent contractor — but generally W-2 is cleaner)
5. Establish separate bank account for child (custodial account if minor)
6. Process payroll periodically (weekly, bi-weekly)
7. Maintain time records (timesheets signed by child and parent-employer)
8. Keep work product evidence (photos of work, social posts created, files organized)
9. File W-2 to child by January 31
10. Child files own return if wages > $16,100 (2026) / $15,750 (2025) or has unearned income; otherwise no filing required
11. Child contributes earned income to Roth IRA up to $7,500 (2026) / $7,000 (2025) — separate compounding strategy

## The Roth IRA stacking strategy

Wages paid to child create earned income that allows Roth IRA contributions. A 12-year-old who earns $7,500 and contributes to Roth IRA (2026 limit) has 50+ years of tax-free compounding before retirement. $7,500/year for 5 years compounded at 7% for 50 years = $1.13M tax-free at retirement.

This is one of the most powerful long-term wealth-transfer strategies in the code.

## Common errors we inherit

- **No documentation of actual work** — IRS reclassifies wages as nondeductible gift
- **Child too young for claimed work** — 5-year-old "doing bookkeeping" not credible
- **Wage rate way above market** — IRS reclassifies excess as gift/distribution
- **S Corp paying child directly** — FICA owed (may still be worth it but math changes)
- **Wages paid into parent's account** — destroys the strategy; must be paid to child
- **No state child labor compliance** — labor law violation separate from tax
- **Child's wage commingled with allowance** — undermines arm's-length nature
- **No timesheets** — fails contemporaneous documentation standard
- **Using same wages for child's expenses parent would've paid anyway** — IRS questions actual transfer

## Stacking opportunities

- **Spousal employment**: similar strategy for spouse
- **Roth IRA for child**: contributes earned income, decades of tax-free compounding
- **Section 127 educational assistance**: business can pay for child's education with separate $5,250/year exclusion if employee qualifies
- **Multi-child families**: strategy multiplies — each working child adds capacity

## Post-OBBBA and current law impact

OBBBA 2025 did not directly amend children-employment mechanics. Core strategy continues.

### Indirect OBBBA impacts

**CTC at $2,200 (2026)**: OBBBA made the Child Tax Credit $2,200 permanent. Doesn't directly affect children-employment strategy but relevant to overall family tax picture for clients with dependents.

**Standard deduction at $16,100 (Single 2026)**: Child's standard deduction (for earned income) is the greater of $1,350 or earned income + $450, up to $16,100. Allows wages up to the standard deduction floor without federal tax.

**§199A interaction**: Children's wages reduce S Corp net income → reduces QBI base slightly. Net effect usually positive (wages shift from parent's bracket to child's 0% or 10% bracket).

**SALT cap / AGI implications**: Children typically have low/no AGI impact individually. Strategy primarily benefits parent's AGI / tax bracket.

### FICA treatment for family businesses

Unchanged by OBBBA:
- Child under 18 employed by parent's SOLE PROPRIETORSHIP or PARENT-OWNED PARTNERSHIP: NO FICA
- Child under 21 employed by parent's sole prop/partnership: NO FUTA
- Child employed by parent's S CORPORATION: Standard FICA/FUTA applies (entity is separate legal person)
- Child employed by parent's C CORPORATION: Standard FICA/FUTA applies

Family business tax efficiency significantly higher for sole prop/partnership than S Corp.

## Interaction with other strategies (expanded)

### S Corp restriction — notable

If client's business is an S Corp, children hired directly receive no FICA benefit. To capture the FICA exemption:
- Set up a sole proprietorship (often under a Family Management Company structure) as a disregarded entity
- Children employed by the sole prop
- Sole prop provides management services to the S Corp
- S Corp pays sole prop; sole prop pays children

This structure adds complexity and must be bona fide (real services, arms-length rates). Partner review required for this structure.

### Coordinates with Spousal-Employment

Spouse and children employed together creates payroll complexity but aggregates family income-shifting opportunities. Spouse's wages are standard (FICA applies); children's wages get FICA exemption only in sole prop / partnership structure.

### Non-interaction with S-Corp-Reasonable-Comp

Children's wages are separate from owner's reasonable comp analysis. Owner W-2 is independent.

### Coordinates with Solo-401k-SEP-Comparison

If children are meaningful employees (age 21+ or otherwise creating eligibility), they become eligible employees for retirement plan purposes. Complications:
- Solo 401(k) becomes non-eligible (non-spouse employee present)
- Plan must be regular 401(k) or SEP IRA with coverage
- Typically avoided by keeping children's role and hours below eligibility thresholds OR treating as independent contractors (when truly independent)

### Coordinates with Hiring-Spouse (Spousal-Employment)

Separate W-2s and separate Solo 401(k) eligibility for spouse. Spouse's employment doesn't trigger non-spouse employee issue. Children's eligibility is what matters.

### Non-interaction with QBI

Children's wages reduce S Corp net income which reduces QBI base. Small negative effect. But shifts income to child's bracket — net usually positive family-wide.

## Audit posture

### Risk profile: medium; bona fide employment is the central defense

- **LOW** when child actually performs work, wages reasonable for age/duties, FLSA-compliant
- **MEDIUM** when wages seem high for child's age or role
- **HIGH** when no documentation of actual work performed
- **HIGH** when family management company structure used with minimal documentation

### Audit trigger scenarios

- Large wages to minor children on S Corp or sole prop returns
- No 1099 or W-2 for children reported as independent contractors
- Child "employee" of age 8 performing "consulting work"
- FICA exemption taken but entity is S Corp (not sole prop)
- Family management company with minimal factual basis

### Defense considerations

- **Actual work performed**: documented with dated timesheets, photographs, work product examples
- **Wage documentation**: reasonable rate for age and task (minimum wage compliant; not $50/hr for a 10-year-old doing filing)
- **Payroll records**: W-2 or 1099 filed; withholding appropriate
- **Task list**: what tasks the child performs (content creation, filing, social media, website maintenance, basic bookkeeping)
- **FLSA compliance**: hours and task restrictions for minors
- **Age-appropriate roles**: shown with actual work product

### Statute of limitations

- Standard 3-year §6501 limitation
- §6501(e) 6-year if understatement > 25%
- Re-characterization as disguised gift from parent to child if wages not reflective of real work
