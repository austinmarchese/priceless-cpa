# Late S Election (Form 2553 Relief)

Commonly inherited issue: client intended to be S Corp but never filed Form 2553, or filed it late. Priceless identifies, files for relief, and restores the intended status often retroactive to formation.

## The basic mechanic

Normally, Form 2553 must be filed by:
- The 15th day of the 3rd month of the tax year to be effective for that year, OR
- Any time during the prior tax year for effectiveness in the following year

For calendar-year entities: March 15 of the election year.

Missed deadline = default treatment:
- Corporation: C Corp (double taxation, no pass-through)
- LLC default: disregarded entity (sole owner) or partnership (multiple owners) — still pass-through but no S Corp benefits

Late relief procedures allow the IRS to accept a late-filed Form 2553 as if it had been filed timely, provided specific requirements are met.

## Rev. Proc. 2013-30 — the primary relief procedure

Most relief requests use Rev. Proc. 2013-30. Requirements:

1. **Entity is eligible for S status** (all shareholders eligible, no more than 100, only one class of stock, etc.)
2. **Entity intended to be treated as S Corp** as of intended effective date
3. **Failure to qualify as S Corp was due to failure to timely file Form 2553**, not any other issue
4. **Reasonable cause** for failure and due diligence after discovering
5. **3 years and 75 days** maximum from the intended effective date (time window)
6. **All returns filed consistent** with intended S status (or taxpayer amends to be consistent)

## When this works

- Client has been operating as if S Corp (on QBO, in conversation) but Form 2553 never filed
- Client filed Form 2553 but past the deadline, didn't check "late election relief" box
- Client received IRS correspondence treating entity as C Corp
- New-to-Priceless client whose prior CPA didn't catch the missing election
- Formation in year 1, Form 2553 skipped, discovered in year 2 or 3

## When this doesn't work

- More than 3 years and 75 days past intended effective date (statute has expired)
- Entity doesn't qualify for S Corp status (fatal)
- Returns filed treating entity as C Corp (reflects intention to be C Corp, not S Corp)
- Reasonable cause can't be established
- Entity has generated meaningful income/losses that would need to be restated

## The relief process

### Step 1: Assess eligibility

- Confirm entity meets S Corp qualification requirements
- Confirm intended effective date is within 3 years and 75 days
- Confirm reasonable cause for the late filing exists
- Confirm prior returns reflect S Corp treatment (or can be amended)

### Step 2: Prepare Form 2553 with relief request

Standard Form 2553 plus:

1. Write "FILED PURSUANT TO REV. PROC. 2013-30" at the top
2. Complete all parts of Form 2553 normally
3. Check the box for late election relief in Part I
4. Attach a reasonable cause statement (see below)
5. Attach shareholder consent statements (each shareholder signs)
6. Attach statement that entity and shareholders have treated entity as S Corp

### Step 3: Reasonable cause statement

Common reasonable cause scenarios:

- "Client mistakenly believed entity was automatically S Corp upon LLC formation"
- "Prior CPA failed to file Form 2553 despite engagement to do so"
- "Client filed Form 2553 late due to not receiving EIN in time, not realizing deadline"
- "Confusion between LLC check-the-box election and S Corp election"
- "Discovered only upon engagement with new CPA (Priceless)"

Statement should:
- Explain the specific circumstances of the failure
- Confirm client acted in good faith
- Confirm corrective action taken promptly upon discovery

### Step 4: File with IRS

- Mail Form 2553 with attachments to appropriate IRS service center
- Address varies by state; Form 2553 instructions provide
- Keep copy with certified mail receipt
- IRS typically responds within 60-90 days

### Step 5: Receive IRS determination

- Positive response: IRS accepts late election, entity is S Corp retroactively to requested effective date
- Request for more information: provide as requested
- Denial: rare with Rev. Proc. 2013-30 when requirements met; appeal options available

### Step 6: Address any prior-year return amendments

If any returns were filed as C Corp (or LLC partnership) for years that should have been S Corp:

- Amend prior C Corp returns (remove C Corp tax)
- File S Corp returns (Form 1120S) for the same years
- Amend shareholders' personal returns to reflect K-1 income instead of wages/dividends
- Refund claims flow from the amendments

For years where default treatment was LLC partnership (not C Corp), generally minimal change — pass-through character preserved, but S Corp benefits (reasonable comp optimization, §162(l), etc.) may now apply.

## Value quantification

### Year-forward value

Once S Corp status established, client captures:
- FICA/Medicare savings on distributed profits (vs. SE tax or double tax)
- QBI §199A deduction (20% of qualified business income)
- §162(l) health insurance deduction (if W-2 wages)
- Solo 401(k) employer contribution capacity
- Access to S Corp-specific strategies (reasonable comp, Augusta, etc.)

Typical: $5K-$25K per year.

### Catch-up value (retroactive years)

For years retroactively treated as S Corp, potential refund from:
- Overpaid SE tax (if sole prop default) or overpaid C Corp tax (if C Corp default)
- Additional QBI deduction missed
- §162(l) deduction not previously taken
- Reasonable comp analysis benefits

Typical: $10K-$50K total catch-up, depending on number of years.

## Common errors in relief requests

- **Reasonable cause weak** — generic statements fail
- **Shareholder consents missing** — each shareholder must sign
- **Prior years not consistent** — returns filed as C Corp with no amendment plan
- **Time window exceeded** — beyond 3 years and 75 days, Rev. Proc. 2013-30 unavailable (need alternative procedure, much harder)
- **Entity doesn't qualify** — discovered during relief process (e.g., non-qualifying shareholder)
- **Form 2553 "late election" box not checked** — automatic rejection

## Alternative relief procedures

If Rev. Proc. 2013-30 unavailable:

### Rev. Proc. 2003-43

Predecessor; similar but less favorable. Sometimes used for unusual cases.

### §1362(b)(5) inadvertent termination relief

When S election was validly made but later inadvertently terminated. Different procedure, not for initial late filing.

### PLR (Private Letter Ruling)

For unique situations where revenue procedures don't apply. Expensive (~$30K+ fee), long process (6-18 months). Last resort.

## State elections

Many states require separate S Corp election at state level.

- **Federal-conforming states** (most): federal S status = state S status automatically
- **Separate state election required**: New York (NY-CT-6), Arkansas, New Jersey, others
- **No state income tax**: FL, TX, WA, NV, etc. — no state election needed

When fixing federal late election, also verify state election is in place.

## Post-OBBBA and current law impact

OBBBA 2025 did not amend §1362 (S corporation election) or Rev. Proc. 2013-30. Late S election relief framework unchanged.

### Indirect impacts

**QBI continuity**: §199A made permanent by OBBBA. Late S election establishes the S Corp status retroactively; QBI deduction for the retroactively-corrected years may be available on amended returns. Significant value for clients with retroactive relief windows.

**SALT cap at $40,400 (2026)**: If state PTET election also needs retroactive setup (some states allow retroactive PTET alongside S election), coordinate. Most states don't permit retroactive PTET; plan accordingly.

**Reasonable comp window**: If retroactive S election is approved, W-2 wages for the retroactive years become required. Client likely didn't run payroll during retroactive period. Rev. Proc. 2013-30 approval typically requires that the client treat past distributions as retroactively re-characterized — but the W-2 / payroll tax implications need careful handling.

## Interaction with other strategies

### Coordinates with S-Corp-Reasonable-Comp

Once late election is granted, reasonable comp analysis applies retroactively to approved years. Typical scenarios:

- Owner took distributions in years 1-3 with no W-2 → IRS treats distributions as partially reclassified to wages for those years
- Priceless computes reasonable W-2 for each retroactive year; back-files corrected W-2s and 941s
- Employment tax catches up; interest and potentially penalties apply

Alternative: Rev. Proc. 2013-30 language sometimes allows "no payroll tax for retroactive years if corrective W-2 reporting done" — depends on IRS examiner discretion. Worth requesting in the relief statement.

### Coordinates with QBI-Optimization

Retroactive S Corp status unlocks retroactive QBI deduction. If retroactive years had business income, amended returns may include QBI (didn't exist on original Schedule C returns because entity wasn't treated as S Corp).

### Coordinates with PTET-Election-By-State

Some states require S Corp status prior to PTET election. If client missed S election, they also missed PTET. Retroactive S election may (state-dependent) permit retroactive PTET. Additional recovery opportunity beyond federal-level relief.

### Coordinates with S-Corp-Basis-Tracking

New basis tracker must be created retroactively for all approved S Corp years. Shareholder contributions, distributions, income pass-throughs all rebuilt into basis schedule. Complex but necessary for future loss deductibility.

## Audit posture

### Risk profile: low for clean 2013-30 cases; medium for complex; high for egregious delays

- **LOW** when late election within 3 years + 75 days of intended effective date AND reasonable cause clearly documented AND all shareholders consistently treated entity as S Corp
- **MEDIUM** when late election > 3 years past but reasonable cause strong
- **HIGH** when late election attempted via PLR for situations outside Rev. Proc. 2013-30 safe harbors
- **HIGH** when IRS has initiated examination and late election is defensive response

### Audit trigger scenarios

- Multiple late elections by same preparer (pattern)
- Late election followed shortly by aggressive tax positions on S Corp returns
- Retroactive elections where some shareholders would benefit and others wouldn't (consent challenges)
- Late election denied at IRS level but taxpayer filed as S Corp anyway
- Step-transaction concerns when entity recently acquired or restructured

### Defense considerations

- **Contemporaneous reasonable cause statement** (not reconstructed after the fact)
- **All shareholder consent signatures** (original, dated)
- **Prior treatment evidence**: tax returns filed as S Corp (even if no election); books kept as S Corp; owner treatment as such
- **Reason for delay documented clearly**: preparer oversight, administrative issue, specific circumstance
- **Tax consequences calculation**: what's the net impact if election granted vs. denied?
- **PLR backup**: if outside safe harbor, consider PLR ($10K-$30K filing fee but binding)

### Statute of limitations

- Rev. Proc. 2013-30 has specific 3-year + 75-day window from intended effective date
- Beyond window: PLR required (§301.9100 relief)
- Amended returns for each retroactive year subject to normal statute of limitations (typically 3 years from original filing)

## Deliverable points (documentation skill handoff)

When late S election appears in a client memo:

### In the narrative memo

- **Recommendation statement**: "File Form 2553 with Rev. Proc. 2013-30 late election relief language by [date]. Retroactive effective date: [date]. Corrects [number] years of Schedule C treatment to S Corp."
- **Why quantification**: Savings from S Corp treatment × retroactive years. If payroll tax recovery included: net savings = (SE tax avoided - new W-2 FICA - penalties and interest).
- **Trade-off statement**: Amended 1040s required for retroactive years. Back-filing of W-2s and 941s. Interest on reclassified amounts. Potential audit exposure if relief not granted.
- **Action items**: Form 2553 filing; reasonable cause statement; shareholder consents; amended returns preparation
- **Deadline**: Based on specific case; typically within current tax year

### In the Excel model

- **Multi-year retroactive tab**: per-year reconstruction of S Corp treatment vs. actual Schedule C filings
- **Strategies tab**: row for "Late S Election + Retroactive Treatment" with quantified benefit
- **Actions tab**: election filing; amended returns; W-2/941 corrections
- **Notes tab**: reasonable cause summary; relief procedure used; prior-year reconstruction methodology
