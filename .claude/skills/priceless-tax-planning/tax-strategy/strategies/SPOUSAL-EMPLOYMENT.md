# Spousal Employment in the Business

When done legitimately, employing a spouse creates retirement plan capacity, legitimizes business expenses, and (in some structures) reduces overall employment tax. When done badly, it's a frequent audit issue.

## The basic mechanic

Owner's spouse becomes a W-2 employee of the business. Result:

1. Spouse receives W-2 wages, deductible to business
2. Spouse can establish their own Solo 401(k) (if business is sole prop / spouse-of-parent partnership) or contribute to existing plan
3. Spouse can contribute to own HSA if HDHP coverage and 55+ for catch-up
4. Spouse's business travel (when actually working) becomes deductible
5. For S Corp owners: spouse can be paid via accountable plan reimbursement for legitimate expenses

## When this works

- Spouse genuinely performs work (administrative, bookkeeping, customer service, marketing, operations)
- Wage is reasonable for the work (similar to what you'd pay an unrelated person)
- Documentation supports legitimacy
- Spouse has time available to perform the work

## When this doesn't work

- Spouse is a token employee (no actual work)
- Spouse has full-time job elsewhere with no time for this work
- Wage massively exceeds market value for the work
- Spouse never trained for the role and produces nothing

## FICA treatment

Less favorable than children. Spouse's wages from spouse's business are subject to FICA/Medicare in most cases:

| Employer | FICA on spouse |
|---|---|
| Sole proprietorship of other spouse | YES (subject to FICA) |
| Single-member LLC of other spouse | YES |
| Partnership of two spouses | YES |
| S Corp | YES |
| C Corp | YES |

The §3121(b)(3)(B) FICA exemption for spouses is limited and rarely applicable. Generally, plan on paying FICA on spouse's wages.

This means the FICA cost of paying spouse must be offset by other benefits (retirement plan capacity, etc.) for the strategy to net positive.

## Solo 401(k) for spouse — the highest-value lever

If the business is a sole proprietorship or partnership of spouses only, the spouse can establish their own Solo 401(k) (or be added to existing plan as second participant).

For each spouse: $23K employee deferral + 25%/20% employer contribution = up to $69K combined per spouse (plus $7,500 catch-up if 50+).

Couple ages 50+: combined retirement contribution capacity = $69K + $7,500 + $69K + $7,500 = $153K annually shielded.

This is materially more capacity than one-spouse Solo 401(k) ($76,500 max).

For S Corp owners, the structure works similarly: spouse becomes W-2 employee, gets own employee deferral capacity, S Corp makes employer contribution based on spouse's W-2.

## Wage rate

Reasonable for the actual work. Not "I'll pay my spouse $200K/year for 5 hours/week of light admin." Common rates for typical roles:

- Bookkeeping/admin: $25-$60/hour depending on skill
- Marketing/social: $40-$80/hour for capable
- Operations management: $60-$120/hour for high-skill
- Sales: commission + base reasonable for industry

If spouse has professional credentials (MBA, CPA, JD) and uses them for the business, wages can be higher to reflect that.

## Health insurance interaction

When spouse is W-2 employee:
- For S Corp: spouse not a >2% shareholder (just employee), so health insurance can be paid pre-tax via cafeteria plan if S Corp has one
- This is materially more favorable than the >2% shareholder treatment for the owner-spouse
- Family coverage (covering owner, spouse, kids) provided to spouse-employee can be tax-free fringe benefit to spouse and deductible to S Corp without owner's >2% shareholder limitations

This is a meaningful wrinkle that often reduces total family healthcare cost.

## Implementation steps

1. Identify legitimate role for spouse based on business needs
2. Document job description
3. Set wage rate appropriate for work
4. Set up payroll for spouse (or add to existing)
5. Establish accountable plan if spouse will incur business expenses
6. Issue W-2 annually
7. Establish or contribute to retirement plan based on spouse's wages
8. Maintain time records and work product evidence
9. Annual review of role, wages, and effectiveness

## Common errors we inherit

- **Spouse W-2 with no actual work** — IRS reclassifies as gift / distribution
- **Wage rate massively above market** — IRS reclassifies excess
- **Spouse "working" 60 hours/week while also employed full-time elsewhere** — credibility problem
- **Spouse W-2 issued but no payroll runs** — paper-only employment fails
- **Solo 401(k) contribution made for spouse without spouse being legitimate participant** — disqualifies
- **Family travel deducted as business when spouse contribution is minimal** — disallowed personal portion
- **Health insurance through spouse's W-2 not coordinated with §162(l) for owner** — confusion in setup
- **Accountable plan reimbursements without proper substantiation** — taxable wages

## Stacking opportunities

- **Solo 401(k) for both spouses**: doubles retirement capacity
- **DB plan including spouse**: spouse's wages and age factor into plan design; can dramatically increase combined contribution
- **HSA for spouse**: separate $4,400 self-only / $8,750 family (2026) capacity if HDHP coverage; $1,000 catch-up if 55+
- **Children employment**: spouse can supervise children-employees; combines well
- **Travel and meals legitimization**: business trips where both spouses work become substantively deductible

## Post-OBBBA and current law impact

OBBBA 2025 did not amend spousal employment rules directly. Core strategy continues under existing §162(a), §3121, and §401 frameworks.

### Indirect OBBBA impacts

**§199A QBI**: Spouse's wages reduce S Corp net income → reduces QBI base. For non-SSTB above threshold, spouse's W-2 counts toward 50% W-2/UBIA ceiling (increases deduction capacity). For SSTB or below threshold, net neutral.

**§401(a)(17) comp cap at $360K (2026)**: Each spouse has independent comp cap for retirement plan purposes. Dual spouses maxing at $360K = $720K of effective §401(a)(17)-cap-relevant compensation.

**SECURE 2.0 Roth catch-up mandate (effective 2026)**: Each spouse evaluated independently for the $150K W-2 threshold. One spouse may hit it; other may not.

**Estate and gifting implications**: OBBBA made $15M per-taxpayer estate exemption permanent. Dual-spouse retirement plan balances compound substantially over decades.

## Interaction with other strategies (expanded)

### Creates separate Solo-401k-SEP-Comparison opportunity

Spouse's W-2 establishes separate Solo 401(k) eligibility for spouse:
- Separate §415(c) limit ($72,000 2026 + catch-up)
- Separate $24,500 employee deferral
- Separate Backdoor Roth IRA annual $7,500 (+$1,100 catch-up)

Combined household pre-tax retirement max (both spouses age 50+) 2026:
- Spouse A Solo 401(k): $80,000
- Spouse B Solo 401(k): $80,000
- Total: $160,000 retirement pre-tax
- Plus HSA family: $9,750 (with both age 55+ catch-ups)
- Plus backdoor Roth for each: $17,200
- **Combined household pre-tax / Roth: $186,950/year**

### Creates separate Defined-Benefit-Overlay opportunity

Separate actuarial calculation for spouse based on spouse's age and W-2 comp. Dual DB participation can double the DB contribution capacity for the household.

### Coordinates with S-Corp-Reasonable-Comp

Each spouse needs independent reasonable comp analysis. Spouse's role, duties, time commitment, market rate different from owner's.

### Coordinates with QBI-Optimization

Both spouses' W-2s count for QBI W-2 limit (above-threshold non-SSTB). More flexibility in meeting 50% W-2 or 25% W-2 + 2.5% UBIA requirement.

### Coordinates with Hiring-Children

Orthogonal — spouse and children are separate employees. Both can appear on S Corp payroll.

### Coordinates with Health-Insurance-S-Corp-162L

If both spouses are >2% shareholders, both can use §162(l) deduction. However, family health insurance typically covered under one plan (both spouses); deduction doesn't duplicate.

### Triggers controlled group concerns

If spouse has ownership in ANOTHER business with employees, §414(c) controlled group or affiliated service group rules may aggregate. Could create coverage issues for Solo 401(k), etc.

Screen carefully for spouse's other business interests before setting up dual-employment structure.

## Audit posture

### Risk profile: medium; spouse must be bona fide employee

- **LOW** when spouse performs real work, reasonable comp, normal employment procedures
- **MEDIUM** when spouse's role or hours are minimal
- **HIGH** when spouse's wages appear engineered purely for Solo 401(k) capacity
- **HIGH** when spouse has no documented work product or role description

### Audit trigger scenarios

- Spouse wages equal to exactly what maxes Solo 401(k) deferral capacity
- Spouse has no timesheet, task log, or work product
- Spouse wages significantly above RCReports benchmark for the role
- Spouse's role unclear ("assistant," "consultant," no details)
- Payroll recently started coinciding with Solo 401(k) setup
- Controlled group with spouse's other employer missed

### Defense considerations

- **Spouse role description**: clear job duties, reporting relationships
- **Timesheet or task log**: demonstrates actual work performed
- **Reasonable comp benchmark**: RCReports or equivalent for spouse's role
- **Payroll records**: W-2, 941, proper withholding
- **Written employment agreement**: even if informal, documented
- **Evidence of actual work product**: emails, deliverables, hours logged

### Statute of limitations

- Standard 3-year §6501 limitation
- Re-characterization risk: wages re-classified as gift or disguised distribution
