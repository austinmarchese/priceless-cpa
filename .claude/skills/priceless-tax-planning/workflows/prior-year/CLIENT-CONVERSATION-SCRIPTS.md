# Client Conversation Scripts — Prior Year Amendment Opportunity

These are not word-for-word scripts. They are structural frameworks with specific language for the hardest moments — avoiding criticism of prior CPA, quantifying recovery honestly, disclosing risk, and closing on engagement letter same-call.

## Opening framework

The opening matters more than anything else. The goal is to frame this as **proactive discovery**, not **criticism of prior work**. Tony's direct communication style matches this framing — no hedging, just facts.

### Standard opener

> "I want to walk you through something I found while reviewing your TY 20XX return as part of our firm's standard portfolio review. There's a specific item [or: there are a few items] where I believe we can recover meaningful tax you already paid — somewhere in the range of $X to $Y. I want to explain what I found, what the process looks like, and what it would cost. Then you can decide if you want to pursue it."

Three things this opener does:
1. **Frames as standard firm process** — not a special pitch; this is what Priceless does
2. **Quantifies the recovery range** — sets expectations honestly with a band, not a single number
3. **Offers agency** — "you can decide" — not high-pressure

### Avoid these openings

- "Your prior CPA missed..." → sets adversarial tone; may trigger client defensiveness
- "You won't believe what I found..." → theatrical; undermines professional tone
- "This is easy money..." → dismisses the real work involved; misrepresents risk
- "I can save you thousands..." → promissory; creates expectation Priceless may not meet

## Explaining what was found

Use plain language for the specific item(s). Example for the most common finding (PTET credit):

### Example: PTET not claimed

> "In TY 20XX, your S corporation made an election in [state] to pay state income tax at the entity level. That's a federal tax strategy — the entity pays the state tax, and the federal government lets the business deduct that state tax, which you can't do on your personal return because of the $10K SALT cap.
>
> Your entity made the payment correctly: $X in [state] PTET. But the credit for that payment, which should have flowed to your personal return as a state tax credit you paid, didn't get claimed on your 20XX personal return. It's on the K-1 but wasn't transferred to the right line of your personal return.
>
> To fix it, we file an amended personal return — Form 1040X — claiming the credit. [State] will refund $X, plus interest. The federal side isn't affected because the entity already took the deduction. This is a mechanical fix — clean, clear, and low-risk."

### Example: QBI aggregation missed

> "You have three pass-through businesses: [A, B, C]. The §199A QBI deduction rules let owners 'aggregate' related businesses for the purpose of maximizing the deduction — there's a specific test under IRS regulations. When I looked at 20XX, your return treated each separately, which limited the QBI deduction to [$X]. Under aggregation election, I calculate it should have been [$Y] — a difference of [$Z] in deduction, worth about [$Z × marginal rate] in tax.
>
> The aggregation election is retroactive via an amended return. The technical work is confirming each business meets the aggregation test — same ownership, same accounting period, related trade or business, not an SSTB. I believe all three qualify here, but we'd document each carefully before filing."

### Example: Real Estate Professional Status (REPS) not elected

> "You and [spouse] own rental real estate — the [property] and [property]. In TY 20XX, you had approximately [$X] in rental losses reported as 'passive' on your return, which couldn't be deducted against your [wages / business income] because of the passive activity loss rules.
>
> However, based on what I know about [your / spouse's] real estate activity — the hours spent managing properties, dealing with tenants, working with contractors — you likely qualify as a Real Estate Professional under IRS §469(c)(7). That status converts rental losses from passive to non-passive, meaning they become currently deductible.
>
> For 20XX, if we can substantiate [>750 hours and >50% of personal services in real estate trades/businesses] for [you / spouse], we can amend the return to release those $X in losses. The tax impact is approximately [$Y].
>
> The challenge is documentation. REPS is audited more aggressively than most positions. Before I recommend amending, I need to see whatever records you have of your real estate time — calendars, emails, property management logs, anything. If the documentation is solid, this is worth pursuing. If it's thin, I'll tell you honestly, and we won't file."

## Presenting the recovery range

Always present a **range**, never a single number. Real CPAs don't promise specific refund amounts — the IRS has the final word.

### Specific language

> "My estimate is that we recover between [$low] and [$high] across [year(s)]. The low end assumes [conservative assumption]. The high end assumes [optimistic but defensible assumption]. My central estimate is about [$mid]. I'm comfortable representing this to the IRS, but I want you to hear the range so you know this isn't a promise of a specific refund."

### What the range includes

- Federal tax refund (with interest from original due date)
- State tax refund if applicable
- Interest: IRS pays interest on refunds from the original due date (recent rate ~8% annually)
- Does NOT include: any penalties or fees already paid (separate question)

### What to caveat

- IRS may reject position → refund not paid
- IRS may reduce amount → partial refund
- Processing time: 8-16 weeks typical, longer for complex issues
- State processing time varies by state

## Pricing conversation

Have this conversation explicitly. Don't hide the fee structure.

### Contingent fee presentation

> "I'll structure this two ways — you choose.
>
> **Option A — Contingent fee**: You pay nothing until the IRS processes the refund. If the refund comes through, our fee is [25%] of the amount recovered. If the IRS denies the claim, you owe us nothing. This aligns our incentives — we only get paid when you get paid.
>
> **Option B — Flat fee**: [$3,500] regardless of outcome, paid at engagement. You keep 100% of whatever refund comes through. This is better for you if you're confident in the recovery and want to maximize net.
>
> For your situation, I'd recommend [option] because [reason]. Both are legitimate options."

### When to recommend contingent vs. flat

- **Contingent** when: client is skeptical, recovery is uncertain, relationship-building with new client, or recovery is large ($30K+) where 25% is still good fee for client
- **Flat** when: recovery is mechanical and near-certain, client has liquidity, or recovery is small ($5K-$15K) where contingent fee would be tiny for Priceless

### Avoid

- Hourly billing on amended returns unless exploratory (triggers scope anxiety)
- Contingent % above 30% (feels predatory; Circular 230 considerations)
- Bundling current-year planning with amendment fee (confuses two different engagements)

## Risk disclosure — critical

Tony's style aligns with direct risk disclosure. Do not soft-pedal risks. This is both good practice and required under Circular 230 §10.34.

### Standard risk language

> "I want to be direct about what could go wrong.
>
> **Audit risk**: Amended returns are reviewed more carefully by the IRS than original filings because you're explicitly saying 'something changed.' That's not the same as being audited — most amendments process without examination — but the risk is elevated. For this specific claim, I'd rate the audit risk [low / moderate / high] because [specific reason].
>
> **State follow-on**: If we amend the federal return, [state] will likely trigger a corresponding state amendment. That could be a second refund [good] or a second review [neutral]. We handle it, but I want you to know it happens.
>
> **Cascade to other years**: If this issue also existed in [other years], amending [year] may reveal it and require us to address other years. We can either amend those too (more work, more recovery) or take no action on them. I recommend we decide that now, not after the fact.
>
> **What I won't do**: If I find something that looks recoverable but the position is aggressive or the documentation is thin, I'll tell you and not file. I won't put your name on a position I can't defend."

### For high-risk positions (REPS, §1202, aggressive §199A)

> "This particular position is more aggressive. Here's specifically what I'm concerned about: [specific concern]. Before I would file an amended return claiming this, I would want [specific documentation or evidence]. If we have that, I'll proceed. If we don't, my recommendation is to not file — you're not out anything except my review time, and we go forward with cleaner documentation so you can take this position in future years properly."

## Answering predictable questions

### "Will this trigger an audit?"

> "Honestly, I can't promise it won't. Amended returns get reviewed more carefully, full stop. But the distinction is: will the IRS accept the amendment and pay the refund, vs. will the IRS open a broader examination of your return. For this specific issue, I expect the former because [reason]. If the IRS does audit, I handle that separately under a representation engagement. But I'm not in the habit of filing amendments I can't defend, so I'm not expecting an audit here."

### "Why didn't my prior CPA catch this?"

> "A few possibilities. Sometimes it's a judgment call and they made a different call. Sometimes they had limited scope — tax return prep, not tax planning — and the missed item required specific planning work that wasn't in their engagement. Sometimes it's a specific rule change or regulation clarification that wasn't widely known at the time. I try not to guess at someone else's work; I just identify what I see and can support."

### "How long will this take?"

> "Prep on our end: about 2-3 weeks from engagement letter signed. IRS processing: 8-16 weeks typical, sometimes longer if they request additional info. State processing varies — some states are fast, others take 6 months. Total: plan on 3-6 months from engagement to refund deposit."

### "What if the IRS rejects it?"

> "Three possible outcomes: (1) IRS accepts the amendment and issues refund, most likely. (2) IRS has questions and requests additional information — we respond, and refund proceeds. (3) IRS denies the claim. If they deny, we have two options: let it go, or appeal. Appeal is additional work and additional fee, but the original contingent fee terms would adjust to reflect that. In my experience with this specific type of claim, denial is [uncommon / moderately likely / possible]."

### "Can I just leave it alone?"

> "You absolutely can, yes. The statute of limitations on this amendment expires [date], so if you do nothing, the money stays with the IRS permanently. Some clients decide that's fine — the effort, the complexity, the audit risk aren't worth it to them. I'd rather have that conversation now than pressure you into something you're unsure about."

## Closing the engagement letter

Don't let the call end without either (a) engagement letter sent same day, or (b) specific follow-up date agreed.

### Engagement letter components for amended return work

Either:
- Send electronic engagement letter within 4 hours of the call
- Schedule follow-up call within 5 days if client needs time to review

Engagement letter specific to amendment engagement should include:

- Tax year(s) covered
- Specific issues being amended (not generic "review of prior returns")
- Fee structure: contingent % or flat fee
- Estimated timeline
- Documentation requirements from client
- Authorization for IRS communication (Form 2848 Power of Attorney)
- Risk disclosure language (also in 1040X filing)
- Scope limitations — what's NOT covered (other years, other issues, audit defense)

### Separate from current-year engagement

Amended return engagement should be a **separate SOW** from current-year planning engagement:
- Different scope
- Different fee structure potentially
- Different team mix
- Different timeline

Don't combine unless client specifically requests a bundle.

## Declining client without damage

Some clients will decline. Keep the door open.

> "Understood. Here's what I'll do: I'll document what I found in your file, so if you change your mind before the statute of limitations expires in [date], we can pick back up. No pressure, no cost. And if you'd like me to prioritize these items in future years going forward — meaning, make sure we're maximizing these deductions in this year's planning — just let me know."

## Post-engagement

Once engagement letter signed:
- Immediately move to RETURN-LEVEL-CHECKLIST Stage 5 (preparation)
- Set calendar reminder for IRS response expected by date
- Log in DECISION-LOG-TEMPLATE.md with engagement status
- Note any cascade work for follow-up engagement letters

If client declines after conversation:
- Log in DECISION-LOG-TEMPLATE.md with declined status and reason
- Note SOL expiration date for future outreach trigger
- Continue relationship on current-year work

## Notes on tone

Tony's dry, flat communication style is well-matched to this framework. The conversation should:
- Be factual, not salesy
- Acknowledge complexity, not minimize it
- Present options, not push decisions
- Disclose risks, not hide them
- Price transparently, not obscure structure
- Close decisively once opportunity is qualified

Avoid:
- Hype ("huge refund!" "you won't believe this!")
- False urgency (beyond legitimate SOL timing)
- Criticism of prior CPA
- Promises about specific outcomes
- Emotional appeals
- Defensiveness if client pushes back
