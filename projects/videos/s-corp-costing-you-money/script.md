## Script

### Hook (~0:00-0:50)

Your S-Corp is costing you money, not saving you money. I know you set it up specifically to save on taxes. That's why we do it. But here's what nobody told you: the S-Corp election itself doesn't save you anything. The management of the S-Corp does. If nobody's managing it, you're probably paying more than you would have as a sole proprietor.

I'm Anthony, I run Priceless CPA, and we manage S-Corps for a living. Over 200 clients, and I'd say at least half of them came to us because one of these five things was already happening. Business owners doing $300K, $500K, a million plus. They elected S-Corp two or three years ago, and their tax situation is now worse than when they started. Penalties, surprise tax bills, compliance problems their CPA never warned them about.

So today I'm walking you through the five ways your S-Corp is probably costing you money right now, and some of these I'd bet your CPA has never mentioned.

### Anchor 1: Payroll Issues (~0:50)

Let's start with the biggest thorn in the side of S-Corps: payroll. Specifically, four flavors of payroll problems.

**Sub-point A: No payroll at all**

The most common thing I see when someone comes to us: they elected S-Corp and never set up payroll. They're just taking distributions. They think they're saving money on self-employment tax. What they've actually done is created two or three years of back employment tax liability.

You have to pay yourself a reasonable salary or the IRS will determine one for you. There's a well-known tax court case, an S-Corp owner who paid himself zero salary for two years and took everything as distributions. The IRS reclassified all of it as wages, then assessed back employment taxes on the entire amount. This isn't obscure law. The IRS has been saying since 1974, under Revenue Ruling 74-44, that if you are an S-Corp officer providing services and you take distributions instead of paying yourself a salary, those distributions are wages.

If your CPA let you run your S-Corp for two years with zero W-2 income, that's not a strategy. That's compliance failure.

**Sub-point B: Underpaying salary**

Now, even when payroll is running, many owners are underpaying. The leading case here is the Watson case, David E. Watson, a CPA who paid himself $24,000 a year while taking over $200,000 a year in distributions. He got audited. The IRS brought in an expert, ran a compensation analysis using industry surveys, and determined his reasonable salary should have been $91,044. Pretty easy win for the IRS.

Here's the math that matters. The IRS reclassified $67K of distributions as wages. That's roughly $10,000 of FICA, plus a 20% accuracy penalty, plus interest. You're easily looking at $15,000 or more in additional liability for a single year from one common mistake.

**Sub-point C: Overpaying salary**

Here's the mirror problem nobody talks about: what if your salary is too high? If you could've reasonably paid yourself $90,000 and you're paying yourself $150,000, you're voluntarily paying FICA on $60,000 you didn't have to. That's about $9,200 a year in unnecessary payroll tax. Over three years you just gave away $27,000 because nobody ran the numbers.

Now at this point you've probably noticed that salary pays payroll tax and distributions don't. So you might think that lower salary always means less tax. You'd be wrong.

There's a thing called the QBI deduction under Section 199A, where higher W-2 wages can actually increase your deduction. At high income levels, the increase in payroll taxes is less than the tax savings from the larger deduction. So at high income levels, a significantly higher W-2 salary can save you money.

Let me show you the math. On a $1.4M business income pre-salary, assuming no other wages and the owner pays themselves $150K, their QBI deduction caps out at $75K. Bump that W-2 to $400K, and the deduction jumps to $200K. The extra payroll tax on the $250K of added salary is about $7,500. The tax savings from the extra $125K deduction are at least $46K, before even calculating state differences.

And just like that, $40K in net tax savings. A basic calculation that saves this business owner 2.8% of their income from taxation. That's not small. Wars have been fought over a 2% tax.

**Sub-point D: Payroll penalties**

Last, payroll can be compliance hell. The IRS does not play about this. States usually have to be registered separately, often with a different department, often with their own taxes you now have to deal with. California alone has three different payroll taxes.

Here's how the IRS sees it. When you withhold taxes from payroll, FICA and income tax, that money doesn't belong to you. It's held in trust for the government. If you fail to send it, even because you used it to cover a slow month or your bookkeeper dropped the ball, the IRS can come after you personally. Not just the S-Corp. You. Your house, your accounts, your assets. And the penalty is not 10%, not 25%. It's 100% of what's owed, dollar for dollar.

Messing around with payroll as an S-Corp owner can completely offset your tax savings, or put you in real trouble. Use a payroll software, Gusto, ADP, or work with a CPA who manages it ongoing.

One more thing. When you go from Schedule C to S-Corp and set up payroll, your rate of receiving letters from the government will increase 5 to 10x. Way more registrations, way more filings. Most of these letters aren't mistakes you or your CPA made. They're dumb administrative issues or state requirements. Pure, time-consuming compliance work. Be ready for that yourself, or appreciate your CPA a little more for shielding you from this paperwork hell.

### Anchor 2: Late Filing Penalties (~7:30)

Now before we go further, I want to hit a smaller but completely preventable one: late filing.

The S-Corp return is due March 15, not April 15. You can extend six months to September 15, but an extension or a filing has to happen by March 15 every single year. Miss it, and the penalty right now is around $250 a month, per shareholder.

We had a client come in last year, three shareholders, forgot the March 15 deadline, filed in September. That's five months late, times three shareholders, times $250. About $3,750 in late filing penalties on a return that owed nothing. And because they had already used their first-time abatement on a prior year, the IRS wouldn't waive it.

If you're a serial late filer, abatement is not coming to save you. Put the March 15 deadline on every calendar you own.

### Anchor 3: State and Local Issues (~9:00)

Here's one that catches people completely off guard: your S-Corp might not even work the way you think it does, depending on where you live.

New York City doesn't recognize the S-Corp election at the city level. You pay the general corporate tax. That's 8.5% to the city of New York. S-Corp advantage at the city level? Gone.

We had a client who moved their operating business to Manhattan and didn't realize this. First year in NYC, they got hit with an extra five-figure city tax bill their prior CPA never flagged. Not because the prior CPA did anything wrong on the return. Because nobody told them not to make the election in that jurisdiction in the first place.

Tennessee is the same story. Doesn't recognize the S-Corp at the state level. You're a regular C-Corp for state purposes, and franchise and excise tax applies. California hits you with a minimum $800 or a 1.5% tax on net income at the entity level. S-Corp still usually makes sense in California, but remember you now owe tax at the business level on top of your personal. Massachusetts, Pennsylvania, Illinois, more states where S-Corps get taxed at the entity level.

We always tell people upfront when an S-Corp doesn't make sense in their jurisdiction. That's not a sales conversation. That's an advisory conversation. If your CPA didn't tell you about the California net income tax or the NYC corporate tax before you made the election, they weren't advising you. They were processing paperwork.

### Anchor 4: Compliance Traps (~11:00)

Now here's the one that keeps me up at night. You can actually lose your S-Corp election and not even know it. It terminates automatically. No warning, no letter.

How does that happen? Let's say you transfer stock to your brother's LLC. Boom. Terminated. You add your non-resident spouse to the ownership. Boom. Terminated. You bring in a corporate investor. Boom. Terminated. You create different distribution rights for different classes of shareholders. Boom. Terminated.

There are strict requirements for who can own an S-Corp and how funds are distributed. Mess one up, and now you're a C-Corp. That means double taxation. The company pays tax on income, and you pay tax again when it comes out as a distribution. Your S-Corp savings, gone.

And under Section 1362(g), there's a five-year lockout before you can re-elect. Up to five years of double taxation because of a paperwork decision nobody reviewed. DIYing changes to your S-Corp is a bad idea. There's a lot at stake and a lot that can go sideways.

**Sub-point: Fringe benefit and health insurance mistakes**

One more trap inside this category, and it's brutal. If your S-Corp pays your health insurance but it's not reported correctly on your W-2, you lose the deduction entirely.

Here's how it's supposed to work. If you own more than 2% of the S-Corp, your health insurance premiums have to be included as wages on your W-2 in Box 1. Subject to income tax, not subject to FICA. Then you take a self-employed health insurance deduction on your personal return. If step one doesn't happen, step two can't happen. You lose the above-the-line deduction entirely.

We literally had to teach this to a payroll company once. The company filing the W-2s didn't know how important this was. And it goes deeper. If your S-Corp offers a cafeteria plan, greater-than-2% shareholders can't participate. If you do anyway, the entire plan can be disqualified for every employee, not just you. Group life, HSA, and most fringe benefits come with similar restrictions. If you offer benefits, you need someone who actually knows the rules.

### Anchor 5: Basis (~13:50)

Okay, the last one. This is the sleeper topic that your CPA probably isn't tracking, and it's the one that determines whether the money you take out of your S-Corp is tax-free or taxable.

It's called basis.

Here's how it works. When you start your S-Corp, your basis is whatever you put in. Invest $50K, your basis is $50K. Every year, your share of company income increases your basis. Business losses decrease basis. Distributions decrease basis. Simple enough so far.

Here's where it gets real. Under Section 1368, distributions from your S-Corp are tax-free, but only up to your basis. The second you take out more than your basis, that excess is a capital gain. You owe tax on it, and most people have no idea it happened until tax time.

Let me walk you through a real example. Owner puts in $50K to start. Year one, $80K of income passes through. Basis is at $130K. Takes $70K in distributions. Basis at $60K. Fine so far. Year two, $60K of income passes through. Basis goes to $120K. Takes $90K. Basis at $30K. Still fine. Year three, $40K of income passes through. Basis goes to $70K. Owner takes $100K in distributions. That last $30K exceeds basis. Capital gain. At 15%, that's $4,500 in unexpected tax. At 20% plus the 3.8% net investment income tax, it's over $7K they did not see coming.

How does this even happen? Taking money out you didn't put in and didn't earn?

Debt. Everybody uses debt. Credit cards, vehicle loans, SBA notes. Nobody thinks, "How much debt does my S-Corp have?" But debt does not increase your basis. If you have credit card debt, vehicle loans, or any other loans in the S-Corp, you are at high risk of over-distributing your basis.

Businesses in any industry can run into this. E-com owners fueling growth on credit cards, watch out. Gas station and car wash operators expanding inside an S-Corp with acquisition debt, watch out. Buying a car wash or gas station can be a powerful tax play outside an S-Corp because of all the depreciable assets. But acquiring those same assets inside an S-Corp using debt, you're playing yourself. You can't take money out without triggering tax. You can't take the losses because loans don't give you basis. No basis, no losses, no tax-free distributions.

A few years ago, the IRS started requiring Form 7203 on your personal return if you own an S-Corp. Specifically to track basis. Your CPA should be updating this number every single year. If they're not, you have no idea whether the money you are pulling out of your business is tax-free or generating a tax bill nobody is seeing coming.

We started doing a client's tax return, guy owns a car wash. His old tax preparer wasn't filling out Form 7203 properly. She was just leaving a six-figure over-distribution on his return, year after year, for years. Until we found it. We're talking $30,000 plus in taxes per year on a guy not making a million dollars of income. That's material. That's life-changing if the IRS catches it.

If your CPA hasn't brought basis up, ask them why. This is exactly the kind of thing a reactive CPA misses and a proactive CPA tracks quarterly.
