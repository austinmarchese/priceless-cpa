## Anchor 1 — Payroll Issues

Let's start with the biggest thorn in the side of S-Corps, which is payroll issues. Specifically the four flavors of payroll issues

### Sub-point A: No payroll at all

The most common thing I see when someone comes to us: they elected S corp and never set up payroll. They're just taking distributions. They think they're saving money on self-employment tax. What they've actually done is created two or three years of back employment tax liability. You have to pay yourself a reasonable salary or the IRS will determine that for you.

There was a tax court case, a guy named Joseph; he was a CPA, by the way. Sole shareholder paid himself zero salary for two years, took everything as distributions. The IRS reclassified all of it as wages, assessed back employment taxes on the entire amount.

This isn't obscure law. The IRS has been saying this since 1974 (Revenue Ruling 74-44) that if you are an s corp officer providing services and you take distributions instead of paying yourself a salary, those distributions are wages.

### Sub-point B: Underpaying Salary

Now, even when payroll is running, many of them are underpaying, and there's an important example on this one too.

David, who also was a CPA, not making this up. He paid himself $24,000 a year while taking over $200,000 a year in distributions. Got audited. IRS brings in an expert, runs a compensation analysis using industry surveys, and determined that his reasonable salary should have been $91,000.

Pretty easy win for the IRS. Now here's the important math. The IRS replaced $67k of those distributions as payroll, that's roughly $10,000 of FICA taxes plus a 20% accuracy penalty plus interest. You're easily looking at $15,000 or more in additional tax liability for a single year. By making this common payroll mistake.

### Sub-point C: Overpaying Salary

Now here's a mirror problem that needs to be talked about: what if your salary is too high?

If you could've been reasonably paying yourself $90,000 and you're paying yourself $150,000, you're voluntarily paying FICA on $60,000 you didn't have to. That's about $9,200 a year in unnecessary payroll tax. Over three years you just gave away $27,000 because nobody ran the numbers

At this point, you may have noticed that salary pays payroll taxes and distributions don't pay payroll taxes. So you might think that lower salary always leads to less taxes. You'd be wrong. There's a thing called the QBI deduction under Section 199A where higher W-2 wages can actually increase your deduction. At high enough income levels the increase in payroll taxes is less than the tax savings from the increased deduction so especially at high levels of income it could make so much sense to have a significantly higher W-2 salary.

As an example, on a 1.4M business income pre-salary, assuming no wages besides the business owner pays themselves $150k. Their QBI deduction caps out at $75k. If they bump it up to $400k W2, that deduction increases to $200k. The extra payroll taxes on the $250k of added salary is about $7500, while the tax savings from the extra 125k deduction are at least $46k without even calculating state differences. That's an extra 40k in tax savings. A basic calculation that saves this business owner 2.8% of his income from taxation. That's not small, wars were fought over 2% taxes.

### Sub-point D: Payroll Penalties

Last but absolutely not least for payroll issues is probably the biggest obstacle or reason why S Corps can be a massive headache for S Corp owners. Payroll can be absolute compliance hell. The IRS doesn't play about this. States generally have to be registered with, often it's a different department and they have their own taxes that you now have to deal with. Like CA has like 3 different payroll taxes.

Here's how the IRS sees it, when you withhold taxes from payroll: FICA income taxes, that money doesn't belong to you; it's held in trust for the government. Now if you fail to send it because you used it to cover a slow month because your bookkeeper dropped the ball, the IRS can come after you personally, not just the s corp. you. your house, your accounts, your assets.
The penalty is not 10%, not 25%. It's 100% of what's owed, dollar for dollar.

Messing around with payroll as an S-Corp owner and incurring penalties can completely offset your tax savings or get you in significant trouble. Setup a payroll software. Gusto, ADP, or work with a CPA who can help you manage it ongoing.

I want you to know that if you go from Sch C to S Corp and setup payroll, your rate of receiving letters from the government will increase probably 5-10x. Because there are so many more registrations and filings you now have to do. If you get flustered by receiving mail from a government, or don't have someone to resolve the notices for you, then S Corps might not be for you. Because oftentimes these letters aren't even mistakes you or your CPA made, it's just dumb administrative issues or requirements. Pure, time consuming, compliance work. Be ready for that yourself or appreciate your CPA more now that you realize we've been shielding you from this paperwork hell.

## Anchor 2 — Basis Issues

Okay let's move out of payroll into a sleeper topic that not enough people are talking about and your CPA probably doesn't track it sufficiently. But this is what determines whether the money you take out of your S-Corp is tax-free or taxable.

It's called BASIS. Here's how it works

When you start your S Corp your basis is whatever you put in. Invest 50k; your basis is 50k. Every year your share of the company's income increases your basis. Business expenses & losses decrease the basis. And distributions decrease the basis. Simple enough so far.

Here's where it gets more complicated and where it really matters. Under Section 1368 distributions from your S Corp are tax-free but only up to your basis. The second you take out more than your basis, that excess is a capital gain. You owe tax on it and most people have no idea that it happened until tax time.

Let me walk you through a real example. Let's say starting a business, the owner puts in $50K. Year one, $80,000 of income passes through. Basis increases to $130K. Takes out $70K in distributions; now the basis is at $60K. Fine so far

Year two, $60,000 of income passes through; basis goes to 120K. Takes 90,000; basis drops to 30K. Still fine

Year 3: $40,000 of income passes through. Basis goes to $70,000 but the owner takes $100,000 in distributions. That last $30,000 that exceeds basis, that's a capital gain. At 15% that's $4,500 in unexpected tax. At 20% plus the 3.8% net investment income tax over $7k, they almost certainly didn't see it coming because nobody told them.

How does this happen? Taking money out that you didn't put in and didn't earn?

Really it only happens when you're using debt but everybody uses debt. Everybody has credit cards, vehicle loans; like everybody uses debt and so no one thinks about, "Oh how much debt does my S-Corp have?" So let me just say this and I really want to reiterate this important point.

If you have credit card debt, if you have vehicle loans, if you have any other loans whatsoever, you are at high risk of over-distributing your basis in an S-Corp. You need to be very careful with this concept.

Businesses in any industry can run afoul of this. But I see alot, E-Com guys that fuel their growth on credit cards, look out for this. I've seen gas stations or car washes expand within their S-Corps, taking out a bunch of debt to grow. This could be a very powerful tax strategy outside of an S Corp because buying car washes or gas stations have a ton of depreciable assets. But when you acquire these depreciable assets in an S-Corp using debt, then you're playing yourself because you can't take any money out of the S-Corp without unnecessary taxes. You can't take losses out of this S-Corp because when you get loans it does not increase your basis. If you don't have the basis, you can't take the losses or the distributions are taxable.

And just a few years ago the IRS started requiring a form on your personal return if you own an S-Corp, called Form 7203. it's specifically to track this issue: basis issues. Your CPA should be tracking this number every single year and if they're not, you have no idea whether the money you are pulling out of your business is tax free or generating a tax bill that nobody is seeing coming. There's a lot of ancient accountants that still never learned how to prepare Form 7203.

We started doing Car Wash Guy's tax return and his little old tax preparer unfortunately wasn't filling out Form 7203 properly. She just was leaving a massive red flag of like six-figure over distributions on his tax return for years until we found it and addressed it. That could be like $30,000 plus taxes per year and this guy is not making a million dollars of income. This is a material amount that he would get penalized. So be super careful with this one. Just work with someone who knows what they're doing.

## Anchor 3 — Late Filing Penalties

Did you know that the tax deadline actually can be March 15?

Yes if you have a pass-through entity (partnerships or S-Corps), your tax return is due March 15th. You can extend it for six months to September 15 but do not forget that an extension or a filing needs to happen by March 15th of every year. The penalty usually changes every year but it's currently around $250 a month per shareholder. If you have three shareholders and you forget your extension and then file in September, then you're paying what, four or five thousand dollars in late filing penalties? Ouch. Fortunately there is such a thing as first-time abatement, but it's for the first time. If you're a serial late filer, you're not going to get that stuff abated. And I already talked about payroll late-filing penalties but I want to emphasize again how much higher payroll late-filing penalties and late payment penalties can be. There are a lot more deadlines you have to keep in mind when you have S-Corps.

## Anchor 4 — Compliance Traps

You can actually lose your S Corp election and not even know it. It terminates automatically. No warning, no letter the moment you stop being a C corp.

How does that happen?

- Let's say you transfer stock to your brother's LLC. Boom! Terminated.
- Let's say you added your non-resident spouse to the ownership. Boom! Terminated.
- Or maybe you brought in a corporate investor. Boom! Terminated!
- Maybe you even created different distribution rights for different shareholders. Boom! Terminated.

You have strict requirements for who can own S Corps and how to distribute funds from the S Corps. Mess it up now you're a C-Corp. That means double taxation. The company pays tax on income and you pay tax again when it comes out as a distribution. That just ruined the s corp tax savings. Plus under Section 1362G there's a five-year lockout period before you can re-elect S-Corp status. It's up to five years of double taxation because of a paperwork decision nobody reviewed. DIYing changes to your S-Corp is not a good idea. There is a lot at stake and a lot that can go wrong if you mess it up.

### Sub-point B: Fringe benefit / Health Insurance Mistakes

Moving on we literally once had to teach this to a payroll company before. The company responsible for filing W-2s didn't know how important this was on a W-2. If your S-Corp pays your health insurance but it's not on your W-2, you're losing the deduction.

Here's how it's supposed to work:

1. If you own more than 2% of the S-Corp, your health insurance premiums have to be included as wages on your W-2 and Box 1, subject to income tax but not subject to FICA.
2. You take a self-employed health insurance deduction on your personal return.

If step one doesn't happen, step two can't happen. You lose the above-the-line deduction entirely

We see this so frequently and it can get even deeper from there. If you have a cafeteria plan, then certain greater than 2% shareholders can't participate and if you do, the entire plan can be disqualified not just for you but for every employee. There are a lot of rules around benefits and health insurance that you have to be aware of. Restrictions related to group life, HSA, all of these benefits if you offer them.

## Anchor 5 — State and Local Issues

Moving on, this is a big one. Your S Corp might not even work the way you think it does, depending on where you live. New York City doesn't recognize the S Corp election at the city level so you're paying general corporate tax. That's 8.5% taxes to the city of New York. S Corp advantage? Basically entirely gone.

Tennessee? Same thing, doesn't recognize it. You're a regular C-Corp for state purposes. Franchise and excise tax applies.

California hits you with a minimum of $800 or 1.5% tax on net income at the entity level. S-Corp still usually makes sense but in these states but you have to remember that you're owing taxes now at the business level with an S-Corp. Massachusetts, Pennsylvania, Illinois. there are more states where you can owe taxes at the state level with your s corp. Be aware of this. We always tell people upfront when an S-Corp doesn't make sense in their jurisdiction. that's not a sales conversation; that's an advisory conversation. If your CPA didn't tell you about the California net income tax or the NYC corporate tax before you made the election, they weren't advising you or they were advising your poorly.
