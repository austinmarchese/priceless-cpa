## Script

### Hook

The two biggest expenses in your life are housing and taxes. Today I'm going to show you how to use one to shrink the other.

I've helped clients write off close to $20,000 a year in home-related expenses saving them nearly 5 figures on their tax bill. And no, it's not by writing off your whole house. Every strategy I'm about to walk through is documented, defensible, and sitting right there in the tax law.

### Intro

I'm Anthony, I own Priceless CPA where we manage tax planning for over 200 business owners, and I see the same mistakes with home deductions every single week.

### Context

Here's the thing most people get wrong about writing off your house. They either go way too aggressive, claiming half their home is an office when it's clearly 1 room, or maybe they don't claim anything at all because somebody told them the home office deduction is a red flag. Both of those are wrong. The IRS doesn't hate these deductions. They hate poorly documented ones. It's crucial you get this right, because with this information you can get a deduction for payments you're already making and you can organize these strategies to be audit-proof. Tax savings is pure profit. Every strategy I'm about to show you, I'm going to give you the math AND what you need to have in your file if the IRS ever asks. That's what separates a tax filing from a tax plan.

But here's the bigger picture. This is an important takeaway from this video before we even get into the strategies. The best tax incentives in this country are not for W2 employees. The tax code is built to reward two types of people: business owners and real estate investors. That's who gets access to the deductions, the exclusions, the deferrals, the stuff that actually moves the needle. And it's not a loophole. It's designed that way. The government wants people starting businesses, creating jobs, and investing in property. If you're doing those things, the tax code is set up to reward you. Your job is to actually claim what you're entitled to, with proper documentation, instead of leaving it on the table.

So let's start with the first advantage you get as a business owner that your W-2 friends don't: the home office deduction.

### Point 1: Home Office Deduction (Section 280A)

There's two ways to take this deduction, and oftentimes taking the lazy option is the wrong choice.

First is the simplified method: $5 per square foot, max 300 square feet. That's a max deduction of $1,500. Easy to claim, low audit risk, and it usually leaves a ton of money on the table.

Because the other method is not capped at $1,500. I've seen 15k+ home office deductions, using this one, the actual expenses method.

This is where you take the square footage of your dedicated office, divide it by total home square footage, and apply that percentage to your real housing costs: rent if you're renting, or mortgage interest, depreciation and property taxes if you own, along with insurance, utilities, repairs, etc.

Let's run the math. Say you've got a 2,000 square foot home. Dedicated office, 400 square feet. That's a 20% business-use percentage. Your annual housing costs, mortgage interest, taxes, insurance, utilities, maintenance, let's say that's $30,000. Twenty percent of $30,000 is a $6,000 deduction. At a 35% combined tax rate, that's roughly $2,100 in actual tax savings.

Compare that to $1,500 under the simplified method. Four times the deduction just by doing the calculation.

Now the rules: the space has to be for exclusive and regular business use. A kitchen table doesn't count. A guest bedroom where you also host your in-laws doesn't count. But a converted spare room with a desk and no bed? That counts. I've had flower shops write off their customized garage space as business use of home, resellers write off the portion of their home that they store inventory, legit exclusive and regular use.

And a quick note on depreciation. The actual expense method includes a depreciation component, which is valuable, but it creates a recapture event when you sell the home. That means you will pay a little bit of taxes based on how much you depreciated if and when you sell the home. Don't let that scare you off. Just make sure your CPA knows you're claiming it so they can plan for it.

### Point 2: The Augusta Rule (Section 280A(g))

So that was strategy one for your business owners. Here's one that may shock you if you own a home and especially if you also own a business. It's been a rule since 1976 and almost nobody uses it right. Let me explain the Augusta Rule, section 280A. Named after Augusta, Georgia, home of The Masters. The original idea was that homeowners near the golf course could rent their homes during the tournament without reporting the income. But the provision applies to everyone.

Here's how it works: you can rent out your personal residence for up to 14 days per year, and that rental income is tax-free to you personally. But what if you were also renting it out to your own business so that the business paying the expense is now a legitimate rental expense deduction? That's right. Follow me here. Your business pays yourself rent. Your business deducts it; you receive the rental payments tax-free. That's not a loophole; that is Section 280A.

Let's say you rent your home to your S-Corp for 12 days: one day a month for board meetings, planning sessions, annual reviews, and company meetings. Let's say the fair market rate is $1,000 a day. That's $12,000. Now your S-Corp deducts $12,000 of taxable income. Therefore, you are receiving $12,000 personally tax-free, and at a 35% combined tax rate, the net tax savings is about $4,200.

Now here's where people mess this up, and I need you to hear this part.

Documentation. This strategy lives and dies on documentation.

- You need fair market rental comps, like go on Airbnb or some office rental website and put rates for a home like yours in the area; screenshot them and keep them.
- You need a written rental agreement between you and the business.
- You need a legitimate business purpose for each day, like a board meeting, strategic planning session, company meeting, annual review.
- You need meeting minutes or an agenda, like an attendee list.
- You need to actually do the payment from the business to you as a landlord (check, wire, ACH, not just a made-up entry on your accounting ledger).

And if you do it for 15 days, that changes everything, because if you go over 14 days, the entire tax treatment flips and you report all the rental income. Stay at or under 14 days.

You see, the people who get in trouble with this strategy and the reason why people will call out this strategy as illegitimate is because they see people that are charging like $5,000 a day for a house that would rent on Airbnb for $300. Or they're using all 14 days with no documentation, no meeting minutes, no legitimate business purpose. That's how you give a perfectly legal strategy a bad reputation, so if you follow the rules, you keep the documentation, then this is completely defensible.

So we got $6,000 from the home office. We had $12,000 from the Augusta rule, and let me show you two more ways your house is working for you. One you might already know, but there's one that most people don't think of as a strategy at all.

### Point 3: The Baseline + The Home Sale Exclusion

**Itemized Deductions: Mortgage Interest and Property Taxes**

First, the foundation. You've probably heard of itemizing your deductions, but the game has changed since the one big beautiful bill. If you're itemizing, and now most business owners should be, you're already getting mortgage interest deducted on up to $750,000 of your mortgage balance, and now state and local taxes on up to the $40,000 SALT cap. That increase in SALT cap seriously increased the amount of people that qualify for itemizing. Those are personal deductions on Schedule A, like the home office and the Augusta rule. Those are best as business deductions stacked on top. This is an important one that incentivizes home ownership and is important not to miss, especially now that we get to write off more property taxes.

But here's where it gets interesting because there's one more piece of the tax code connected to your house that most people don't think of, but is actually an effective and repeatable strategy.

**The Home Sale Exclusion (Section 121)**

Section 121: When you sell your primary residence, you can exclude up to $250,000 of capital gains if you're single, $500,000 if you're married filing jointly. No tax on that gain, zero. The requirement is that you have to have owned and lived in a house for at least two of the last five years.

Now think about what that means: you buy a home, live in it for two years, the market does its thing, and you sell with, let's say, $300,000 in appreciation. Married filing jointly, that entire gain is tax-free. Take that equity, roll it into the next house, live there for two years, do it again. Like, there's no limit on how many times you can use this exclusion.

Some numbers say you buy a house for $500k. A couple years later, it's worth $750k. A lot of appreciation, but it's a great market, right? You sell $250k in gain tax-free. The next one at $750k, a couple years later, it's worth $950k. Another $200k in gains tax-free. In under five years, you sheltered $450,000 in gains from tax at long-term capital gains rates that could be $70,000 to $100,000 in taxes you never pay. You just keep upgrading your house as you're able to afford it.

Like, I'm not saying you should move every two years just for the tax benefit; this is a lifestyle decision. If you're already someone who moves periodically or wants to continue to upgrade, relocate, or even downsize, understanding this exclusion turns every move into a tax event in your favor.

Doing this intentionally can make a big difference in your life, and here's the part that ties it all together. While you're living in each home, you're stacking the home office deduction, maybe even the Augusta rule, on top. Your house is saving you money every year while you live in it and when you sell it.

Don't forget what I said about the depreciation recapture, though, because that part does not qualify for the gain exclusion. An example would be if you wrote off $10,000 of depreciation under business use of home for your personal residence. If you then go to sell and try to claim the 121 exclusion, you are still going to have to recapture that $10,000 of depreciation.

### The Full Math

Let's put it all together. A business owner filing jointly with the 400,000 net income and a 600,000 home, and of course a home office. His home office deduction using the actual method is about $6,000 in deductions, roughly $2,100 saved per year. Added the Augusta rule 12 days at $1,000, $12,000 in deductions, about $4,200 saved per year.

Then the mortgage interest and property taxes: let's say he is paying 6% on a 600,000 home, $36,000 in mortgage interest plus, let's call it another $4,000 in property taxes. That's $40,000 of itemized deductions right there; that's about another $3,000 to $4,000 in tax savings.

By organizing this information and filing it properly on your taxes, that's $10,000 in tax savings every year you're living in the home.

And then when you sell up to $500,000 in gains tax-free, that's not a deduction; that's income that simply never gets taxed.

And that's why your house is a tax strategy on these two timelines:
- Annual savings while you live in it
- Massive exclusion when you sell

Most people are only using one strategically. Some aren't using either. I told you at the top the two biggest expenses in your life are housing and taxes. You just saw how to use the housing to save on the taxes on every timeline that matters.
