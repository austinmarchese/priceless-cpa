# Why The Rich Don't Do Tax Strategy

The wealthiest clients I work with don't do tax strategies, at least not the kind that make viral content on Instagram. No hacks, no loopholes, no schemes. And there's a reason for that - one many people find out the hard way.

Most business owners still think tax strategy is about finding clever tricks. The more creative the more you save. The internet, social media, conferences: they're all selling that idea.

The truth is the opposite. The most sophisticated people I work with aren't chasing clever strategies. They're doing a handful of well-documented, well-structured things that hold up under scrutiny and they're doing them precisely.

The people who get burned, they're usually doing crazy strategies that they were convinced are not tax fraud when it is or they might even be doing legit tax strategies but they're doing them wrong and the IRS has specific rules, some of them nearly 100 years old, for testing whether your plan is real or just paperwork. If it's paperwork they can reclassify the whole thing and send you a bill that's bigger than the taxes you were trying to avoid.

So today I'm going to walk you through the five most common rules that the IRS uses to test your tax plan. Any strategy that runs afoul of any of these rules should talk you out of doing that tax strategy.

## Section 1: Substance over form

The Substance Over Form Doctrine. The IRS doesn't have to accept the legal form of your transaction. If what actually happened doesn't match what you put on paper, the IRS goes with what actually happened. This is the oldest and most foundational rule in tax law.

Here's an example. Evelyn Gregory owned a company. That company held stock with about $133,000 in another company. She wanted to own that stock in her personal name instead of her company's name but if her company just handed it to her, she'd owe full income tax like a paycheck. So she created the brand new corporation, transferred the stock she wanted into it, then immediately dissolved this new corporation and took the stock on paper. It's a corporate reorganization; it's tax-free but the corporation existed for six days: no employees, no business, no operations, created on a Monday, dissolved by Saturday.

The Supreme Court said you checked every box; you followed every technical rule but the question is whether what you actually did, setting aside the tax angle, was the thing the law was designed for. A real reorganization involves a real business activity. This was not a real reorganization; this was just a set of otherwise irrelevant procedures for her to save taxes.

This is very relevant to today. If you have transactions on the books and you go, "Oh can we just record it this way?" The CPA should first think, "Well what's the actual economic substance of your transactions? Is it a distribution or is it a loan? Right are you receiving income or are you receiving non-taxable transfers? Like what's the actual economic substance? Do you have a related party transaction or are you just making things up?"

The short rule from this Gregory test is: if I remove the tax benefit entirely but I still have created this entity or done this transaction, the answer is no; it's probably vulnerable.

## Section 2: Economic Substance Doctrine

Next is the economic substance doctrine in 2020. In 2010 Congress codified this into law, section 7701, a two-part test, and you have to pass both:

1. The transaction must change your economic position in a meaningful way apart from the tax incentive.
2. You must have a substantial business purpose apart from the tax incentive.

Fail either one, and the whole strategy gets thrown out.

And the penalties are different from anything else in the code:

- 20% of the underpayment
- 40% if you don't disclose
- no reasonable cause exception

This is strict liability. You can't just make up financial transactions that don't have real-life substance.

A nice story about this is David Watson, who was a CPA. He owned a practice at Nest Corp, part of a larger accounting firm that grossed close to $3 million. He paid himself a salary of $24,000 a year but he took over $200,000 in distribution, which the Nest Corp isn't subject to payroll taxes. The IRS looked at this and said, "You have a graduate degree, 20+ years of experience. You work 35 to 45 hours a week at a profitable firm. In what world are your services worth $24,000?"

They brought in an expert. We used actual compensation data and determined his services were worth at least $91,000. The court agreed over $134,000 in distributions got reclassified as wages. He owed about $50,000 in back taxes, penalties, and interest

Now the guy was just trying to save $10K a year in additional payroll taxes and then he ended up losing $50K. The form here said that he had a $24,000 salary. The substance, based on his qualifications, hours, and the market reasonableness, said he should have been paid $91,000. The IRS and the tax court chose the real substance.

So it's a good story for all you S-corp owners who aren't paying yourself enough salary. It's a good story for all of you thinking about doing a tax strategy where there's nothing to it other than the actual tax savings. If there's no real economic activity happening within the strategy, it is vulnerable.

## Section 3: Step Transaction Doctrine

Next is a step transaction doctrine. If you break one plan into multiple steps to make each step look like an independent event, the IRS can glue all the steps back together into a single transaction and tax the combined result. Were these steps really one coordinated plan? If yes then they are one transaction.

Here's the story: say you own a rental property worth 500k, about 200k in equity. You want to access that equity without paying capital gains. Here's the plan:

1. Cash out and refinance, pull 150k. Refinance proceeds aren't taxable; it's a loan.
2. 3 weeks later sell the property and do a 1031 exchange into a new investment property, deferring those gains.

Each move is perfectly legal on its own but the IRS sees the timing. You pulled equity out tax free then deferred the rest through the exchange. They collapse the two steps into one transaction and treat the $150,000 as taxable. Now you owe capital gains on this loan that you already spent

Now there's a version of this where the taxpayer wins the court case. Frederick Lead Commissioner, the refinance had been in the works for two years. There's an independent business purpose. There's documented reasons that had nothing to do with the exchange. That's okay but in a different ruling a taxpayer who refinanced right before a planned exchange with no other reason, he lost.

Here's a very relevant example: if you buy a real estate property, turn it into a short-term rental, and the next year you turn it into a long-term rental, you may have gotten an active loss the first year when it was a short-term. You get the ease of management and the consistent cash flow of the long-term rental without the pressures of the short-term rental.

If you had this intention of converting it from a short-term to a long-term just to save taxes in the first year of ownership of the rental, then you're going to have that overturned; that's illegitimate. If some circumstance arises that changes your investment thesis or investment requirements, you can actually turn a short-term rental to a long-term rental and not be penalized for it. It's all about the intention

So the lesson here is to build time between your steps when you're doing complicated tax planning, weeks, months, definitely not days. Each step needs its own documented business purpose that stands alone. Why did you refinance needs an answer that isn't "because I was about to sell". Don't create a paper trail that outlines the whole strategy as one coordinated plan. Emails and memos saying "first x, then y and z" are exactly what the IRS looks for.

## Section 4: Assignment of Income Doctrine

The fourth rule is the Assignment of Income Doctrine, where income is taxed to the person who earns it. You can't shift it to a different person, entity, or family member through contracts or paperwork, no matter how well drafted. This goes back to 1930 and the Supreme Court opinion of Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes. Holmes wrote the fruits cannot be attributed to a different tree from that on which they grew. You're the tree. Your business is the tree. The income is the fruit. You can't take that fruit under a different tree and pretend it grew there.

Examples would be:

- Let's say a spouse that doesn't actually do any work.
- A court case where a spouse was paid 40k but she didn't actually do anything. Therefore it wasn't deductible because it wasn't legit wages.
- Same with kids with fake jobs. If your 8-year-old earns 12,000 a year for no real work, that's not going to be upheld.
- Same with any other family entities. You are putting all of your income through a business and trying to arbitrage by putting it in the name of your parents but they don't actually do any work. That is not going to be seen as legitimate even if you are paying payroll taxes.

So here's what to do about it:

1. For your spouse to find a real role with real duties, document her hours and pay a rate consistent with what you pay a non-family member.
2. Run it through your normal payroll system.
3. Same for your kids; their work must be real, age-appropriate, and documented.

For income through entities, the contract should be with the entity and the entity should direct and control the work.

The test for all three. If the IRS walked in and asked, "Show me what this person actually does," could you show them?

## Section 5: The penalty cascade

Last let's talk about the penalty cascade that can come from strategies gone bad. Say a strategy saved you $100,000. IRS audit three years later disallows it. First the savings disappear. You owe the original $100,000 then the penalty of $40,000 because it didn't disclose. Three years of interest, $15,000 to $20,000, total is $155,000 to $160,000. Your $100,000 in savings just cost you $160,000. You are 60 grand worse off than doing nothing.

That real threat might make it not worth doing a tax strategy for you

On the Extreme end, a story: a CPA and attorney sold over $1.3 billion in fraudulent conservation easement deductions. The CPA got 25 years. The attorney got 23. Remember the IRS criminal conviction rate is over 97%.

I've read articles of dentists that got involved in tax fraud trusts and got 6 years in jail. He wasn't even a tax pro just a guy that went along with alot of BS on tax returns.

Now every year the IRS publishes the dirty dozen. The schemes are actively targeted:

- conservation easements
- microcaptive insurance companies
- social media tax hacks

When a scheme gets enough attention it becomes a listed transaction. It's mandatory disclosure. You're not avoiding scrutiny on these transactions; you have to invite scrutiny on these transactions to prove that they're legit

So before you implement any strategy, ask:

- What happens if the IRS audits this three years from now?
- Can I explain every decision?
- Is every entity real?
- Is every number documented?
- Does every step have a purpose beyond the tax benefit?

If any answer is "I'm not sure," have that conversation with your CPA before you file, not after you get a notice. Your CPA really should be thinking about this before you file on your behalf

## Section 6: What a defensible plan looks like

So let's talk about what a defensible plan looks like.

When you make real substantive transactions and decisions, you can structure yourself to save tons and tons of tax from the IRS. Even if the IRS doesn't like it, there's nothing they can do about it.

An example: assume a holdings case. A family combined two things Congress specifically created for tax savings: special entity for exporters and Roth IRAs. Over seven years more than $5 million ended up in those Roth accounts, like way past normal limits, and the accumulation of these Roth IRAs. The IRS tried to blow it up for this family. The Sixth Circuit Court said no. Congress created both of these tools for tax reduction. The family used both for exactly those purposes so you can't create tools for tax saving and then punish people for using them. Like you can beat the IRS. You just have to be very well documented and smart about how you go about doing tax strategies. Most people selling tax strategies are not capable of beating the IRS.

So let's go over the five question checklist again:

1. Does it change your financial position beyond the tax bill? An S Corp changes your structure but a sham entity that does nothing changes nothing.
2. Does it have a business purpose beyond tax savings? A retirement plan? That builds real well. A scheme that only exists to generate a deduction has no purpose without the tax benefit.
3. Can every step stand on its own? A refinance done 18 months before a 1031 for cash flow reasons stands alone three weeks before the 1031 with no other reason to do it that does not stand up on its own.
4. Does the paperwork match reality? Every entity real and operational, every employee doing real work, every payment supported by actual services.
5. Is everything documented? You have market data for your salary, job descriptions for your family employees, business purpose memos for entities? Documentation isn't just for audit defense; it's what separates planning from just hoping you don't get out of it.

If your plan passes all five, you're using the system the way it was designed.

- S-Corp elections
- defined benefit plans
- cost saves
- qbi deductions
- 1031 exchanges
- depreciation
- family employment

Done right, every one of these changes, your economic position. It has a purpose beyond taxes; it stands up independently, matches reality, and it's documented

So why don't rich people do tax strategies? Alright you got me. They do do tax strategies but they just don't do what most people think of as tax strategies.

They don't chase the cleverest strategies. They don't try to save $10,000 by gaming their salary and end up losing $50,000. They don't form entities that exist on paper and nowhere else. They don't stack three strategies in three weeks and nobody notices the timing. The sophisticated business owners aren't doing schemes;

they may be doing similar strategies that everyone else is:

- SCORPs
- retirement plans
- real estate
- family employment
- business investments

But again they're just doing them with substance, documentation, and precision. If somebody offers them a tax strategy without these things, they say no. That's why they want to do tax strategies. They realize these tax strategies could offer so much but could also end up being so much more of a headache than they're willing to deal with.

That's the difference between a tax strategy that saves you money and a tax strategy that costs you everything
