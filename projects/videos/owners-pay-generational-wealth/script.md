## Script

Every business owner I meet wants the same thing. Generational wealth. 

But here's the thing. You can hit multi-six or seven figures in personal income and still own a business worth zero to anyone but you. There are hundreds of tax traps and structural mistakes that can leave a successful-looking business owner broke.

So in this video I'll walk you through a 4-step system to turn a business that OWNS YOU into a business you can sell or scale to 8 figures. And after working with hundreds of businesses as their CPA and Tax Strategist, I'll cover that 1 number that 99% of them get wrong.

### Step 1: Owner's Pay Is Not Net Income

Here's what I see all the time. Most people just see their net income as their owner pay. Or they think Owner's Pay only matters because they have to set a reasonable salary for their S Corp taxes.

That's the wrong frame. And it's why so many owners walk around thinking their business is profitable when it isn't.

Owner's Pay is not the money left over at the end of the year. Owner's Pay is the fair market cost of the services YOU personally provide to your business. Two completely different numbers.

When you separate them, you get two clear questions instead of one confused one.

Question one: What does my business earn as an operation?

Question two: What am I worth as an operator?

Most owners smash those together and call the total "how much I made." And that's exactly why they end up building a job instead of a business.

Understanding and mastering Owner's Pay is the starting point to building a sellable business that creates generational wealth. Miss this, and you're running a business that's really a job where you're your own tyrannical boss. Get this right, and you unlock everything.

Now here's where it gets uncomfortable.

### Step 2: Key Man Risk (The Concept That Kills Most Business Sales) 

If you ever want to sell your business for real money, the acquirer has to replace YOUR services inside the business. That's a massive cost line to consider, given how much work you probably do. It might be two or more full-time salaries worth. Because it's hard to find employees who can do everything you do.

This is called Key Man Risk.

Key Man Risk is the discount a buyer applies to your business because the operation depends too heavily on you. Solve for it, and you multiply the value of your business. Ignore it, and your business is worth exactly what you're worth to keep working in it. Which is nothing to a buyer.

Because if your business can't operate without you, how will you ever sell it?

Or take a month vacation without it costing you that month's income?

Two simple tests to know if you're the Key Man in your business.

Test one: Can you take a full month off without revenue dropping?

Test two: Would a buyer be paying for the operation, or paying for you?

If you flunk either test, your business isn't unsellable forever. It just isn't sellable yet. And that's the entire game. Turning yourself from the operator the business needs, into the leader the business benefits from.

Here's the hard truth that will change everything. If you make $200K but you put in $250K worth of personal services, your business is unprofitable. It is worth nothing to anyone but you. If a potential buyer had to replace you in that business, they wouldn't make any money from acquiring you.

That's the definition of a job with extra steps.

Now, how do you actually know if you're the Key Man? You calculate your replacement salary. Which brings me to Step 3.

### Step 3: Calculate Your True Replacement Salary 

Here's the exercise that changed everything for me. And it's the exercise I run with every client we take on.

Ask yourself: how much would it actually cost to replace me?

For most owners doing multi-six or seven figures, that number is two or more full-time salaries. Because you're not just the CEO. You're also the head of sales, the head of operations, sometimes the top producer, the finance lead, the hiring manager, and the person clients want to talk to when something goes wrong. Be honest with the count.

Then price each seat at fair market rate. Not a lowball. Actual market. You can't expect someone to come into your business and be as productive as you unless you pay them very well, or unless you're just not that good.

Now here's an important nuance. For tax purposes, paying yourself $60K a year in S Corp owner's comp can make sense. It saves on payroll taxes, and done right, it's within a reasonable comp range for a lot of situations. That's a legitimate tax planning move.

But for business analysis, it's not realistic. Not if you're making multi-six or seven-figure income and working 60 hours a week. What's reasonable for the IRS and what's honest for your enterprise value are two different conversations. Don't confuse the two.

Here's what this looked like in my own firm. And it's not a story I'm proud of, but I'm man enough to admit it.

One year I ended up making $200K from my CPA firm. I worked my ass off that year. When my new fractional CFO asked me how much my owner's pay should be, I told him I paid myself $60K in S Corp owner's comp. But he urged me to determine how much it would really cost to replace all that I do in the business. Sales, ops, delivery, hiring, client relationships, finance, all of it.

I ran the numbers. I provided at least $250K of pure value and services to the firm.

He looked at me and said, "So, your business didn't really make much money last year."

That realization hit me like a ton of bricks. I have employees. I made $200K. What do you mean I didn't make any money?

Yep. My CPA firm was literally unprofitable.

Once you see the number, you can't unsee it. Which forces the question in Step 4.

### Step 4: Fix the Gap. Document, Delegate, Elevate.

Once you've calculated your true Owner's Pay and stacked it against your net income, one of three things is true.

Either your business is genuinely profitable and you should be proud. Congratulations, you have a real business.

Or, revenue is too low for the cost structure and you need to raise prices, cut costs, or both.

Or, and this is where most owners land, the business only works because you're doing the work of two or three people for free. And the fix is not to grind harder. The fix is to stop being the person doing the work.

Three moves.

**Move one. Document everything you do.**

Every task, every process, every judgment call you make in a week. If it lives in your head, it can't be sold. Get it on paper.

**Move two. Delegate against measurable KPIs, not vibes.**

Hire people to own specific outcomes. Not "help me out." Specific numbers they're accountable to. Otherwise you just added payroll and kept all the work.

**Move three. Run the business from the top, not from the middle.**

Dashboards, reporting cadences, weekly accountability meetings. Your job becomes assigning responsibility, deciding on objective KPI goals, and holding your team accountable to hit them. Not doing the work yourself.

We dove deeper into my own numbers after that fractional CFO conversation. My labor costs were high. My software costs were over six figures a year. My revenue was way too low to be spending that much money. I was making up the gap with pure hustle and grit without even realizing it.

As honorable as I want to feel about those character traits, it made me feel like an idiot.

Owning that business was 10x the complexity and stress to make the same income as a solid job.

That's the tipping point where I started to become a true business owner.

I decided I don't want to live like this forever, sacrificing my time and relationships for a decent income and a business that would die the moment I stopped working. It was time for my people and my processes to make money.

All of my time since has gone into developing processes, training, and providing accountability to my people. My job is now to not have a job.

Am I all the way there yet? Absolutely not. I still manage some client relationships. I still have finance tasks, sales and marketing, hiring, team management. Still way too many responsibilities.

But all of those tasks are now being performed with the awareness that my job is to document and delegate everything. To assign responsibilities, create measurement mechanisms, decide on objective KPI goals, and provide accountability to my team to hit those goals.

By running my firm from the top, I can see what's happening across the company and allow my team to create the profit. I can elevate myself from the day to day. I can make strategic decisions to scale to 8 figures and beyond. I can free up my time. And I can lead my firm to create the generational wealth and freedom that lets me have a real relationship, enjoy time with my family, and give back to my community.

In ways I simply couldn't while I was running my business blind and unprofitably.
