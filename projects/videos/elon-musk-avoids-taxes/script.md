The answer is, you're probably not doing anything wrong. You're just playing a different game. The people paying the least in taxes aren't hiding income. They've structured their financial lives so income is recognized on their terms, not the IRS's.

One thing I want to be upfront about before we go further. These moves don't scale to every business the same way. ### Section 1: Move One, Control Your Compensation (2:00-5:30)

Elon Musk takes zero dollars in salary from Tesla. Zero. His compensation is structured as performance-based stock options tied to Tesla hitting specific market cap and operational milestones.

Here's the part people get wrong. They think equity compensation is magically tax-free. It isn't. Let me break this down, because it matters.

When Elon exercised a massive block of his options in 2021, the spread between the strike price and the market value became ordinary income. That's the $11 billion federal tax bill. His equity wasn't tax-free. It was tax-deferred. He picked the year to pull the trigger.

There are five main flavors of equity compensation, and each one gets taxed differently. Non-qualified stock options trigger ordinary income on the spread between strike and market price at exercise. That's the bucket Elon's 2021 exercise fell into. Incentive stock options defer regular income tax until you sell, with Alternative Minimum Tax possibly hitting at exercise, and qualify for long-term capital gains rates if held long enough. Restricted stock units hit as ordinary income at vesting. Restricted stock with an 83(b) election lets you lock in a low basis at grant so future appreciation comes out as capital gains. We'll throw a comparison table on screen so you can see all four side by side.

And then there's the fifth one, and it deserves its own deep dive, because it can eliminate federal capital gains tax entirely on millions of dollars. Section 1202 qualified small business stock. QSBS. This is one of the most powerful provisions in the entire tax code, and almost nobody talks about it. And it just got a major upgrade.

For stock issued on or before July 4, 2025, the old rules apply. Five-year hold. Exclude up to $10 million or 10 times your basis from federal capital gains tax, completely.

For stock issued after July 4, 2025, under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, it's even better. Tiered structure now. Hold three years, you exclude 50 percent of the gain. Four years, 75 percent. Five years, full 100 percent. And the per-taxpayer, per-company exclusion cap went from $10 million up to $15 million.

Here's where it gets really interesting. The exclusion cap is per taxpayer. Not per company. Not per transaction. Per taxpayer. Which means if you gift QSBS shares to family members before a sale, each family member gets their own cap.

I had a client whose company qualified for QSBS. Before the sale, he gifted shares to his two kids and his grandparents. When the sale closed, all of them sold multi-million dollars of stock each. Every single one of them paid zero federal capital gains tax on their share. Across the whole family, over $10 million in total capital gains recognized. Zero federal tax. That is millions of dollars, shifted from the IRS to his family, completely legally, because we stacked the per-taxpayer exclusions.

That is what "Elon-level" tax savings actually looks like in practice for a real business owner.

Notice what's happening. Equity compensation doesn't eliminate tax. It gives you control over four things.

Timing. You pick the year the income hits.

Character. You steer the income from ordinary rates, up to 37 percent federal, down to capital gains rates, max 23.8 percent with Net Investment Income Tax. That alone cuts a big bill by a third.

Location. If you're a resident of Texas or Florida when the event triggers, no state tax. We'll come back to that in move three.

And optionality. As long as you don't exercise, don't sell, don't vest past, the underlying value compounds untaxed.

So how does this scale to a closely-held business owner? Most entrepreneurs can't pay themselves in stock options. But the principle still applies. Don't over-convert business profits to ordinary income unnecessarily.

The most common over-conversion I see is S-corp salary, but the YouTube version of this strategy gets it wrong half the time. The right S-corp salary isn't always lower. Sometimes it needs to come down to save self-employment tax. Sometimes it needs to come up to capture the QBI deduction, which on its own can swing your income tax bill by tens of thousands. The right number depends on your business type, your total income, and what else is flowing through the entity.

Real ranges from my actual client work. A business owner profiting somewhere between $100K and $400K a year, with intentional comp strategy across salary, distributions, retirement, and reimbursements, will often save five figures in taxes annually. Sometimes more. For someone in the high six to seven figures of income, that same coordinated approach regularly clears six figures of annual savings. Not from any one tactic. From the integration of all of them.

Bottom line on move one. The principle isn't "take stock, avoid tax." It's "control the trigger." That applies whether you're Elon or you're running a $2 million business out of your home office.

### Section 2: Move Two, Buy, Borrow, Die (5:30-10:00)

This is the one everyone's heard rumors about and almost nobody understands correctly. Buy, borrow, die.

Step one, buy assets that appreciate. Stock, real estate, equity in private companies.

Step two, when you need cash, don't sell. Borrow against the asset.

Step three, pass the assets to your heirs at death, where, most of the time, they get what's called a step-up in basis.

Why this works. Loans are not income. When you borrow a billion dollars against your Tesla shares, the IRS doesn't care. It's debt, not a paycheck. Cash in pocket, zero tax.

Reports in 2022 showed Elon had pledged around 88 million Tesla shares as collateral for personal loans. He's not selling stock to fund his lifestyle. He's borrowing against it.

Now here's where step three plays out differently for normal humans versus Elon. Let me walk through step three for normal humans first.

When you die with appreciating assets in your estate, your heirs get what's called a step-up in basis. That's Internal Revenue Code Section 1014. The basis on those assets resets to fair market value on the date of death. If you bought a rental property for $300K and it's worth $900K when you die, your heirs inherit it at a $900K basis. Sell the next day, zero capital gains tax. The $600K of appreciation never gets taxed.

That is one of the most valuable provisions in the entire tax code for anyone passing wealth down to their family.

Now here's why it doesn't work cleanly for Elon. The federal estate tax exemption in 2026 is $15 million per person, $30 million for a married couple. Anything above that gets hit with a 40 percent estate tax at death. Elon's net worth is in the hundreds of billions. He's got an estate that would lose 40 percent at death unless he actively plans around it.

So at his level, the strategy gets layered. Move appreciating assets into irrevocable trusts now, while values are low, so future growth happens outside the estate. Grantor Retained Annuity Trusts, GRATs, to freeze estate value. Dynasty trusts in states without a rule against perpetuities. Generation-skipping transfer tax exemption used strategically. Private foundations. Elon has one, the Musk Foundation. At his scale, those aren't optional tools. They're how you compress a taxable estate that would otherwise get shredded.

Here's the upside for you. If your net worth sits between $1 million and $30 million as a couple, you're in a structurally better position than Elon on this move. You're under the federal estate exemption. You get the full step-up benefit without needing to build a trust-and-foundation labyrinth to access it. You buy, borrow, hold, and die.

The goal, if you're serious about passing wealth to your family, is to pass away with as many unrealized capital gains as possible, under that estate tax exclusion limit. Now, that sounds a little morbid, but you can seriously save a lot of taxes this way. Every dollar of appreciation that goes untaxed at your death is a dollar your family gets to keep.

Let me show you what this looks like in real situations. Two examples.

Example one. Real estate developer. He buys properties, rehabs them, and significantly increases the fair market value through the work he puts in. When he wants to cash out the new equity to fund the next deal, he doesn't sell. He goes to the bank and either refinances or pulls a HELOC based on the updated value. That extracted equity rolls straight into the next property. He's been doing this deal after deal, compounding his portfolio without triggering a single taxable sale. Every one of those properties is sitting there with decades of appreciation waiting for the step-up when he dies. His kids inherit a real estate empire with fresh basis.

Example two. Client near retirement, low income at this point in his life. He'd accumulated close to a million dollars in Bitcoin over the years and wanted to buy a house.

A normal CPA would have said, sell the Bitcoin, pay the capital gains, buy the house. We looked at his total taxable income for the year and saw he had a lot of room inside the 0 percent long-term capital gains bracket. For 2026, single filers pay 0 percent on long-term gains up to $49,450 in taxable income. Married filing jointly, up to $98,900. He had about $75,000 of gains he could realize at 0 percent federal capital gains tax. Federally tax-free liquidation.

For the rest, roughly $300 to $400K, he took a crypto-backed loan. Borrowed against the Bitcoin and used the cash to round out the house purchase.

He bought the house, kept the Bitcoin position intact, and paid zero federal tax on the entire transaction. Zero. The $75K of gains sat inside the 0 percent bracket. The $300 to $400K he borrowed wasn't taxable because loans aren't income. The interest he paid on the loan was significantly less than the capital gains tax he would have triggered on a straight sale.

Same principle both times. Don't force a taxable event. Borrow and hold.

You don't need Elon-level wealth for any of this. You need a few hundred thousand in appreciated assets and a banker who knows what they're doing. But it has to be structured right, which is where most entrepreneurs fall apart.

### Section 3: Move Three, Domicile (10:30-13:00)

In December 2020, Elon Musk moved from California to Texas. A year later, Tesla followed and moved its headquarters from Palo Alto to Austin.

California's top state income tax rate is 13.3 percent. Texas has no state income tax. Zero.

When he exercised that massive block of Tesla options in 2021, triggering the $11 billion federal tax bill, he paid $0 in state income tax on it. If he'd still been a California resident, that single transaction would have cost roughly $1.5 billion more in state tax alone.

That is domicile arbitrage. The tax code doesn't just care what you earn. It cares where you are when you earn it.

Now I'm not saying every entrepreneur should move to Texas. That's a huge life decision. But think about this from a lifestyle angle, because most of my clients have way more flexibility than they realize.

How many people do you know who live in New York and fly down to Florida for a few months every winter? A lot, right? Now flip it.

What if you lived in Florida and flew up to New York for a few months every year instead?

Someone making $1 million in income living in New York City is paying roughly 13 to 14 percent combined state and city income tax. Call it $130 to $140K a year, every year, going to a tax jurisdiction most people never think to question because it's where they've always lived.

Make Florida your domicile. Real residence, real estate, driver's license, doctors, voter registration, the works. You can still spend five months a year in New York. Hit the good months. Spring, summer, early fall. Avoid Florida's worst heat. Same lifestyle you probably already want, and you save $140,000 a year.

That's not a one-time trick. That compounds. Ten years of it is $1.4 million before growth on what you invested it in.

One warning. Establishing domicile isn't vibes. It's days present, driver's license, voter registration, healthcare, clubs, where your primary home is, where your kids go to school. States like New York and California audit former residents aggressively. If you move, move for real.

Beyond personal residence there's where your entities sit. Wyoming, Delaware, Nevada. These states have favorable charging order protections, privacy, and sometimes state-level tax savings. A Wyoming LLC does not save you federal tax. It does protect your assets and your privacy.

And where your real estate sits affects your tax exposure, not just your cap rate.

One client of mine sold a business last year for $8 million. He'd been a Florida resident for 18 months at the time of the sale. If he'd still been in New Jersey, where he'd lived his whole adult life, he'd have been paying into the New Jersey millionaire bracket. Top rate there is 10.75 percent on income over $1 million. State tax on that sale would have come in around $830,000. Zero in Florida. The 18 months he spent establishing real domicile saved him more than $800,000 on a single transaction.

### Section 4: Move Four, Pick the Right Entity (13:00-15:30)

The state your entities are registered in is one decision. The TYPE of entity is a separate decision, and for most entrepreneurs it matters more than where they're filed.

Start with how this works at Elon's scale. Elon doesn't personally pay tax on Tesla's profits. Tesla does. Tesla is a C-corporation, and the corporation pays its own tax bill on what it earns.

But here's what's interesting about how Tesla manages that bill. They reinvest aggressively. Property, equipment, factories, research and development. Tax law lets you depreciate and expense a big chunk of that reinvestment. When depreciation and expense deductions exceed taxable income, the company can be cash-flow positive, growing the business, and still show near-zero on the tax return. Tesla has had years like that. So have plenty of capital-heavy companies. That isn't a loophole. It's literally how the corporate tax code is built. Reinvestment gets rewarded.

You can run the same play at your scale. Starting point is the right entity choice for your situation. Three rules of thumb.

If you run an operating business and you pull most of the profits out as income, an S-corporation is usually the right structure. The S-corp lets profits flow to you while saving self-employment tax on the distribution portion. Most of my profitable service-business clients are S-corps for this exact reason.

If you're a founder building a company that's going to keep reinvesting, raise outside capital, and grow the value of the equity itself, a C-corporation is usually the right structure. C-corps are how you access the QSBS strategy from earlier. Take a modest salary, let value compound inside the company, and exit with up to $15 million per shareholder excluded from federal capital gains tax under Section 1202. That's the Elon-style move scaled down.

If you're investing in real estate, especially partnered with others, a partnership or LLC taxed as a partnership is usually the right structure. Partnerships unlock the depreciation game. Meaningful rental cash flow on one side, depreciation deductions large enough that taxable rental income comes out to zero or even a paper loss that offsets other income. That's why real estate is one of the most tax-advantaged asset classes in the code.

The wrong entity for your situation costs you tens of thousands every year, sometimes hundreds. Most entrepreneurs I meet have an entity their first accountant put in place when the business was small, and nobody's revisited it since the situation changed.

### Common Mistakes (15:30-17:30)

Here's where entrepreneurs go wrong trying to replicate this stuff.

Mistake one. They misuse the borrow leg of buy-borrow-die.

There are two ways to wreck this strategy. First, borrowing against the wrong asset. They pull a $300K HELOC and dump it into crypto or some speculative deal, and when the asset craters 40 percent, they're paying interest on a loan against a shrunken asset. Buy-borrow-die works on assets with a long track record of appreciation. Stocks. Established real estate. Mature businesses. Not gambles.

Second, borrowing against the right asset but spending the money on lifestyle. Borrowing against your rental portfolio to buy the next rental, that's the play. Borrowing against your stock to upgrade the car or fund a vacation, you've turned a wealth-building tool into expensive consumer financing. The asset you bought loses value. The debt does not.

The rule is simple. Every dollar you borrow should fund an appreciating or income-producing asset. If it can't pay for its own interest and then some, don't take the loan.

Mistake two. Their LLC is in the wrong state for their actual life.

I had a client who moved from California to Texas, set up a Texas LLC, and started operating his business out of it. Great. A year later, he moved back to California. He kept the Texas LLC. He kept running the business. Then he called me furious that he owed California income tax and S-corp franchise tax on the business while he lived there. He'd assumed the Texas LLC protected him. It doesn't. California does not care where your LLC is registered. If you, the owner-operator, are a California resident actively running that business from California, California taxes the income. The state of residence follows you. The structure has to match the reality.

Mistake three. They trigger equity compensation without a plan for the tax bill.

Flip side of Section 1. Stock options and RSUs aren't tax-free, they're tax-deferred, and when the deferral ends, you owe tax in cash that year. I've seen entrepreneurs exercise a big block of NSOs, assume they'd sell enough shares to cover the tax bill, and then watch the stock drop 40 percent before filing. Now they owe a huge tax bill on phantom income while holding an asset worth a lot less than they thought. Or they exercise ISOs and don't realize AMT is going to hit them until March. If you're triggering equity comp, model the tax bill before you pull the trigger and make sure liquidity is in place to cover it.

Mistake four. They wait until tax season to think about any of this. By April, every opportunity for that tax year is gone. Every strategy I've described has to be set up in advance. The clients who save the most are doing tax planning in June, not scrambling in March.

### Action Steps (17:30-19:00)

Here's what I'd tell you if you were sitting across from me right now.

First, pull your last tax return. Look at line 1, wages. Then look at what's flowing through on your K-1 or Schedule E. The right ratio depends on your business type, your QBI position, and your total income, so don't just assume lower W-2 is better. Run the full math, or have someone run it for you.

Second, make a list of every appreciating asset you own. Personal real estate. Rental properties. Investment accounts. Business equity. For each one, ask three questions. What's the basis. What's the current value. And do I have a borrowing facility set up against it. If the answer to the last one is no, that's your next conversation with your banker.

Third, if you have meaningful appreciated assets, get an estate plan done. The step-up in basis is the most valuable gift the tax code gives to the middle-wealth entrepreneur. Don't die without an estate plan that lets your family actually claim it.

Fourth, run the domicile question honestly. Where do you actually spend your time? Where are your kids in school? Where are you on December 31? If there's a gap between where you live for tax purposes and where you live for your life, close it.

Fifth, check your entity setup. Are you in the right type of entity for what your business actually does? S-corp for operating profits you pull out. C-corp for founder-built companies that reinvest and exit. Partnership for real estate with co-investors. If your entity was set up when the business was small and nobody's revisited it, that's a six-figure decision waiting to be made.

Sixth, if you're expecting any kind of liquidity event in the next 18 months, business sale, property sale, equity vesting, inheritance, stop reading articles and get professional help. The window to structure around a big event is measured in months, not weeks.

Here's the takeaway. Elon doesn't avoid taxes because he's a billionaire. He avoids taxes because he controls the trigger. He decides when income happens, where it happens, and what character it takes. The unrealized gains he's not pulling the trigger on are compounding in a way the IRS can't touch until the trigger gets pulled.

Every one of those moves has a version that works at your scale. You don't need Tesla. You need a profitable business, some appreciating assets, and the discipline to plan before you execute instead of the other way around.
