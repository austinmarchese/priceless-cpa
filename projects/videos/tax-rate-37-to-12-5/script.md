### Layer 1a: Entity Structure (~1:20-3:00)

First up, entity structure.

One decision. That's all it takes. One structural decision that can mean millions in total tax difference over the course of a career. And most people get it wrong, or worse, they never revisit it after they set it up on LegalZoom when they were making sixty grand a year.

This particular client is a serial entrepreneur. He's got multiple businesses. So here's how we structured it.

Each unique business operates as either a partnership or a single-member LLC. Every one of those is owned by his S Corporation. The S Corp sits at the top as the operating entity, it's the hub.

Then, separately, his investments sit in a different single-member LLC owned by his trust. Not his S Corp. His trust. That's intentional. You want clean separation between operating income and investment income. Different tax treatment, different asset protection profiles, different planning opportunities.

Here's the design principle most people miss. Keep filings and compliance as simple as possible. Every partnership, S Corp, or C Corp you add is another tax return, another set of books, another thing to maintain. Now, single-member LLCs are usually not additional tax returns, they're disregarded entities, which is part of why we use them in this structure. But don't create complexity for the sake of looking sophisticated. Create structure that serves a purpose.

This client has multiple businesses, so the structure is justified. If you're running one business making four hundred grand, you probably don't need four LLCs and a holding company. You need an S Corp and good accounting. Keep it clean.

Every receipt. Every invoice. Full documentation. I'm not talking about shoving receipts in a folder once a year. I'm talking about a system where every transaction is categorized, documented, and reconciled, weekly.

### Layer 2a: QBID Optimization — Section 199A (~7:15-9:45)

Alright, layer two. This is where we start actually saving real money.

Let's talk about QBID, the Qualified Business Income Deduction, Section 199A of the Internal Revenue Code. This is a free six-figure deduction that most high-income business owners either don't know about or aren't maximizing. And I say "free" because it requires zero additional investment. It's just math. It's just running the numbers right.

Here's how it works at high income levels. Your QBID is the lesser of twenty percent of your qualified business income or fifty percent of your W-2 wages. Those are the two limits inside §199A.

Let's walk through it. On three and a half million dollars of net income, the two-sevenths rule, that's just how the math shakes out when you're trying to max the W-2 limit, puts your optimal owner payroll at around one million dollars. At a million in W-2, fifty percent is five hundred thousand. Twenty percent of three and a half million is seven hundred thousand. Five hundred is less than seven hundred, so you're capped at five hundred K in deduction.

Simple enough, right? But here's what most CPAs miss.

QBID is also capped at twenty percent of your total taxable income. That's a different number. So if this client has other losses, maybe from strategic investments, which we'll get to, that bring his taxable income down to, say, five hundred thousand, then his QBID cap is a hundred thousand dollars. Twenty percent of five hundred K.

At that point, to max the deduction at a hundred thousand, you only need two hundred thousand in W-2 wages. Not a million. Two hundred thousand.

So instead of paying payroll taxes on a million dollar salary, you're paying payroll taxes on two hundred thousand. That one adjustment, understanding how §199A interacts with other losses on the return, saves tens of thousands of dollars. Every year.

This is why your CPA can't just run your numbers in a vacuum. They need to see the full picture. Every entity, every loss, every deduction, all at once. That's where the savings live.

### Layer 2b: Retirement Accounts (~9:45-11:00)

Now before you start thinking layer two is only about QBID, you need to consider this next piece.

Retirement accounts.

This client puts seventy thousand dollars a year into a Solo 401(k) with a backdoor Roth conversion, that's §401(k) on the contribution side and §408A on the Roth conversion side. Here's what's interesting. At his income level, he saves enough through other strategies that he actually skips the upfront tax deduction entirely. He doesn't need it.

Instead, he's playing the long game. Let's do the math. Ten years of contributions at seventy thousand a year. Twenty years of growth after that. At a 7 to 8 percent return, you're looking at roughly seven and a half million dollars in a completely tax-free account.

On a multi-million dollar income, seventy grand a year barely registers. It's a rounding error on his lifestyle. But let that compound for twenty, thirty years, and it becomes generational wealth that never gets taxed again.

And here's the bonus most people don't think about. Asset protection. Retirement accounts are generally protected from lawsuits and creditors. If you're a business owner with any kind of liability exposure, and you all have liability exposure, that matters. A lot.

That's layer two. QBID plus retirement accounts. No risk. No complicated structures. Just knowing the rules better than the next guy, and actually running the play.

### Layer 3: Strategic Tax Investments (~11:30-14:30)

Layer three. Strategic investments.

The question we ask every client at this stage is simple. What business or asset class is aligned with your interests and skills that also creates massive paper losses?

For this client, the answer was a trading business. He invested into it and then took an active role managing it. That's the key detail. He didn't write a check to some fund and sit back. He became an operator. That matters for the tax treatment, and I'll come back to why in a second.

The mechanic is losses plus accelerated depreciation. And here's the part most people miss. You want to front-load losses that don't track your actual cash outflows. Under §168(k) bonus depreciation, you're taking most of the deduction in year one, not spreading it over five, seven, or fifteen years.

Follow the math. If you put a hundred grand into an asset and that asset generates four hundred grand in paper losses, that's four times your cash. At a 37% federal rate, that's almost a hundred and fifty grand in tax savings on a hundred grand investment. Your tax savings alone more than cover what you put in. You're in profit before the asset does anything.

Now add the asset itself. If the business generates cash flow that offsets the deferred taxes you'll eventually owe on recapture, you're essentially making money out of zero. That's an insane ROI. You're profitable in year one on an investment that also compounds for decades after that.

Here's why active management matters. When you're a material participant in the business, the losses are non-passive. That means they can offset your other active business income, not just other passive income. That one distinction is the difference between a loss that saves you hundreds of thousands and a loss that sits on a shelf doing nothing for your current year tax bill.

Same mechanic works across asset classes. Real estate. Oil and gas. Equipment-heavy businesses. Aviation. Different industries, different risk profiles, same playbook. You're buying business assets, front-loading depreciation to save on taxes now, while owning something that actually grows in value.

I want to be transparent about this, because a lot of people in my industry aren't. These are real investments with real risk. You can lose money. Depreciation is a timing benefit. You get the deduction now, but if you sell the asset later, there's recapture. The investment has to make sense on its own, without the tax benefit. If the only reason to do something is the tax savings, it's probably not a good investment.

What made this work for this client is that he actually understood the business. He wasn't blindly writing checks to some syndication he found on the internet. He was making informed decisions about an asset he could evaluate and operate.

Even with that risk, doing this right makes a difference of millions. Often tens of millions over a career.

Let me put it simply. When you stack the tax benefits on top of the economic upside, you can turn a ten percent return into a twenty percent return. The tax benefits are that significant. And when you compound a twenty percent return instead of a ten percent return over fifteen, twenty years, that's not a marginal difference. You're doubling how fast your wealth grows.

That can literally be the difference between being worth five million or ten million. Ten million or twenty million. Twenty million or forty million. All because you played the tax and investment game strategically instead of just writing a check to the IRS every year.

That's the difference between strategic tax planning and reckless tax avoidance.
