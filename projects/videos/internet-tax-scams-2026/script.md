### Scam #1: Writing Off Clothes, Watches, Makeup, and Groceries

I'm starting here because it's the most common. And it drives me insane, because I've seen actual CPAs write this stuff off to win business from me.

The pitch: "I'm an influencer, everything is content, so all my groceries, all my makeup, all my clothes, all my Rolex purchases are business expenses."

No.

§162 says business expenses have to be ordinary and necessary. §262 says personal, living, and family expenses are not deductible. Tax court has been extremely clear on this for decades. Clothing you wear in an Instagram Reel is not deductible unless it's a costume or a uniform that's not suitable for ordinary wear. Your Gymshark leggings from your Bulgarian split squat reel? Not deductible. The groceries you eat for lunch? Not deductible. The Rolex you flex on your day-trading course ad? Not deductible.

I've had clients leave me for CPAs who write this stuff off, and then those clients turn around and think I'm the bad CPA. "You just don't understand my industry." Okay. If your definition of a bad CPA is not writing off clearly nondeductible expenses to buy your business, then yes, I'm a bad CPA.

I study tax court cases. I don't just blindly follow what the client wants. I'm not going to sign my name to a return that puts groceries and Rolexes on Schedule C. Feel free to find a CPA who will. But don't lie to yourself about it being actual tax law.

Now, that one's small dollars per client. The next one is where the fees, and the risks, get much bigger.

### Scam #2: Offshore Entities

Another great way to charge taxpayers multi-5-figure fees for a strategy that sounds like what the elite do to protect assets and save taxes.

Now, tax is circumstantial. There are legitimate uses of offshore structures for very large family offices with specific fact patterns. I'm not going to say zero people ever benefit. But the way 99% of people use offshore entities does not save taxes, does not actually protect assets, and does add multi-5-figures per year to their compliance bill for the privilege of believing the lie.

Here's what promoters DON'T tell you.

The pitch is that offshore entities are inaccessible to US courts. That's actually true. But here's the trap. If you ever need those assets to be inaccessible to US courts, they will also be effectively inaccessible to YOU. If you try to access them to hide them from legal liabilities you're going through, that is contempt of court. It's illegal. To bring the assets back into the US, you have to give them up. You can't freely use the money.

What's the value of asset protection if you can't use the assets anymore?

And for tax savings, promoters claim you can defer paying tax on income earned by an offshore entity. Except the moment you want to actually use the money, you're paying the tax. Subpart F, GILTI, and the PFIC rules exist specifically to shut this down for US persons.

Then there's compliance. Form 5471, Form 8938, FBAR. The international reporting alone can add $5K per year or more to your tax prep bill. You cannot use a simple, small-business CPA for this stuff.

There are very different levels of skill and experience among CPAs. There are CPAs who peak at handling books for small businesses. There are CPAs who run family offices for billionaires. Make sure you're listening to those closer to the second camp, especially on offshore strategy. The fact that you haven't been caught yet doesn't mean your assets are protected. It means you're lucky. And you could be that lucky without wasting 5 figures a year.

### Scam #3: Complex Trusts and Charitable Entities

"This is what the Rothschilds do" is the most infuriating hook I see on social media. How do you know? Show us any proof. Literally zero people with valid professional tax credentials are out there claiming they know what the Rothschilds did.

Here's the pitch. These schemes sell complex, discretionary, spendthrift, non-grantor, irrevocable §643 trusts for mid-to-high 5 figures or more. They claim you can defer or avoid taxes on passive income. They claim you can convert business income into passive income so you can defer or avoid taxes on that too. And they dress it up in language so complex, in mental gymnastics so elaborate, that they even convinced me at one point. That was six years ago when I was a brand new CPA with two years of firm experience.

I'm now probably top 10 in the country in terms of studying and understanding this specific strategy. And I'll tell you flat out: it's tax fraud. Even though thousands of people have done it, that doesn't mean it's not fraud. It means IRS enforcement has been incompetent so far.

The IRS has issued formal notices calling these schemes out. Notice 2005-40 and every "Dirty Dozen" list for the last several years have flagged abusive trust arrangements as illegal. Trusts do not defer or eliminate income tax. In plain English: trusts generally pay MORE tax than individuals at the same income level. They have estate tax benefits, absolutely. Not income tax benefits.

Do you want to bet your family's wealth on IRS incompetence being permanent? In the age of AI and automated enforcement? I wouldn't.

Same thing with 508(c)(1)(A) charitable entities. Promoters tell you any business or income-producing activity can qualify under this "church" or "ministry" status and pay zero income tax. That just sounds stupid, doesn't it? §508(c)(1)(A) is a real Code section for actual churches. It is not a fig leaf for your e-com store or your day-trading course. The IRS is very clear on this. I've seen ministers on Instagram promoting this as tax strategy. I don't care who's selling it. If someone is pitching either of these to you, they're an illegal tax scheme promoter. Don't let them convince you.

### Scam #4: The STR Loophole (Legal, But Wildly Oversold)

Last one. And this one is different from the first three.

This one isn't fraud. Unless you flat-out lie about your qualifications. But it's so oversold that I have to include it.

The STR loophole is real. §469(c)(7) and the material participation tests under Treasury Regulation 1.469-5T are legitimate. If you run a short-term rental with an average length of stay of 7 days or less, and you materially participate, you can potentially deduct losses against your other income. That part is true.

Here's what the Airbnb gurus don't tell you.

Do you understand how much work is involved to run a profitable short-term rental while maintaining an average length of stay of 7 days or less?

If you need to hit 80% occupancy to be profitable, you need over 280 days booked. Which means over 40 unique stays a year. Which means 40 cleanings. 40 check-ins and check-outs. 40 different guests capable of breaking or wearing down your property.

If it takes a cleaner 5 hours to clean and restock the property between stays, she's putting in 200 hours a year on your property. And you have to put in more hours than her to hit material participation. Are you ready to be the maid? The property manager? Then possibly still not turn a profit?

I've prepared taxes for over 100 STRs. About 20% of them are actually profitable. The rest lose money.

Tax savings created by losing money aren't a tax strategy. That's just losing money. If you saved $47K in taxes because your STR lost you $120K, you didn't win. You bought a $73K loss and dressed it up with a deduction.

The strategists and Airbnb gurus aren't being honest with you about that math. I am.
