## Script

If you make more than 6-figures, there are hundreds of ways to do your taxes.

But here's the thing. Most people at every income level are paying more tax than they should. Not because they missed one big secret.  Because they're stuck at Level  1 of a 5-level Tax Optimization Strategy that they don't even know about.

So in this video, I'll walk you through the 5 levels of tax optimization that anyone can follow to reduce your taxes to 15% a year. 

These are the exact moves that took me from paying closer to 25% to paying about 15% on a multi-6-figure income and that I've been doing for my clients for the past 8 years as their CPA.

In each level, Ill flag who it applies to and how you can do this yourself.

### Level 1: Know Your Numbers

Applies to: everyone.

Boring and obvious. Also non-negotiable. And yet most 6-figure earners don't actually have this locked. Which is exactly why they're stuck at Level 1 and can't climb. You can't run tax strategy on numbers you don't actually know.

If you don't know how much you're making, you don't know how much to save for taxes. You don't know what strategies fit your income level. You don't even know how much you should reasonably be spending on personal expenses.

For W-2 earners, this is easy. Your pay stub tells you. Your goal at Level 1 is knowing your gross, your net, your withholding, and whether you're on track to owe or refund. That takes 15 minutes a quarter.

For business owners, this is bookkeeping. Real, professional, ongoing books. Not a spreadsheet you touch once a quarter. Not your spouse or uncle doing you a favor. All of the truly wealthy people I know have professional accountants doing their books on a regular basis. Not because they can't do it themselves. Because they know fresh, accurate books are the foundation every other tax move stands on. Save a few thousand a year by having an amateur do your books, and you'll lose 10x that in strategy you can't execute because you don't have real numbers.

If you don't have Level 1 locked, nothing above it works.

### Level 2: Fill the Free Buckets

Applies to: everyone.

The tax code has a stack of accounts specifically designed to shield income from tax. Most people don't fill them. And they don't cost anything extra to use, they just require you to route money you were already saving into the right container.

The main ones.

**HSA under §223.** If you have a high-deductible health plan, this is the best account in the tax code. Triple tax advantage: deductible going in, growth is tax-free, and qualified withdrawals are tax-free. Max it out.

**401(k) or Solo 401(k) under §401(k).** If your employer offers a match, you're leaving money on the table if you don't hit the match at minimum. If you're self-employed, a Solo 401(k) or a SEP under §408(k) is your version.

**Traditional or Roth IRA.** Even at income levels where the traditional deduction phases out, a backdoor Roth still works for high earners.

Here's a real number to make this concrete. Say I made $300K last year and I want to save some for retirement. I throw $70K into a Solo 401(k) and I get about $28K back in tax savings. That's a 40% instant return on a contribution I was going to make anyway.

Just don't make a dumb investment and trade a dollar for 40 cents. The whole point of Level 2 is that these are moves you were going to make anyway. Money you were going to save or spend on healthcare, routed through the right container, comes back to you as tax savings. If the "investment" only exists to chase the deduction, you didn't save on tax. You bought a loss.

W-2 earners can hit Level 2 completely. If you're maxing your HSA, hitting your 401(k) match, and contributing to a Roth IRA or backdoor Roth, you have Level 2 fully locked. That alone puts you ahead of 90% of your peers.

But if you're a business owner, Level 2 is a floor, not a ceiling. Because Level 3 is where the leverage actually starts.

### Level 3: Match Your Entity to Your Income

Applies to: self-employed earners, roughly $100K net and up.

If you're W-2 only, you can skip this level. But if you have any self-employment income at this level or higher, choosing your entity structure and how you pay yourself out of it is one of the most impactful decisions affecting your overall tax rate.

Most Level 3 conversations center on the S Corporation.

If you're operating as a sole proprietor or single-member LLC and you're netting six figures, you're paying 15.3% self-employment tax on every dollar of profit. An S Corp election lets you split your income into reasonable W-2 salary plus distributions. The salary portion still gets payroll tax. The distribution portion doesn't. Done right, that alone saves multi-4-figures to low-5-figures a year at this income level.

But here's what most owners get wrong. They pick a salary number their prior CPA gave them and never touch it again.

Owner's pay is a lever, not a plug-and-forget number. Too high, and you're paying too much in payroll tax. Too low, and you're either flagging yourself for reasonable comp scrutiny with the IRS (they use the factors from Rev. Rul. 74-44), or you're accidentally choking off your §199A QBI deduction, or you're capping your retirement contribution limits.

That's not a strategy. That's compliance. And it's what separates Level 3 done poorly from Level 3 done well.

### Level 4: Manage the Year, Not the Return

Applies to: business owners running an S Corp, mid-6-figures and up.

This is where most business owners have the biggest single leak. Because Level 4 isn't a January meeting or an April deadline. It's a 12-month rhythm.

Three moves inside Level 4.

**Ongoing projections.** Income expectations change. If you set a strategy in January assuming $300K of income, and by mid-year you're pacing $200K or $400K, in both cases you're paying too much tax if you don't adjust. Real tax planning is a running conversation every quarter that updates your projection, your withholding, and your strategy based on where the year is actually going. If your CPA hasn't run a projection since your last return was filed, ask them why.

**Payroll withholding as a lever.** Yes, you can and probably should make quarterly estimates. But you can also just add to your withholdings through payroll, which is far more responsive and far less painful than writing a 5-figure check every three months.

**End-of-year deduction maxing.** I love this one. All of my team and my contractors and my vendors love me around Christmas time. Because I pay bonuses. And I ask if I can prepay next year's services. Here's the logic. A deduction in December gives you tax savings in April. A deduction in January gives you tax savings a year from April. If I'm going to pay these expenses anyway, and I'm growing the business, give me the deduction NOW.

Concrete year-end moves:

- Prepay software subscriptions if the vendor will invoice you now
- Pay Q4 contractor bonuses in December instead of January
- Purchase business assets you actually need before December 31 for §168(k) bonus depreciation
- Buy real estate that actually cash flows (mindful of §469 passive activity rules)

One warning. Don't spend all the cash you need to run the business next quarter, and don't throw it on a credit card and over-distribute your S Corp basis. Over-distributing basis under §1366 creates a taxable event that erases the deduction you just took. If you don't know what that means, that's a separate video, DM me and we'll talk.

Aggressive, yes. Reckless, no.

### Level 5: Master Your Personal Tax Edge

Applies to: high earners, roughly mid-6-figures and up.

Nobody talks about Level 5. It's where I saved myself tens of thousands of dollars last year on its own.

Level 5 is knowing the specific rules of your industry, your filing status, your phase-outs, and the personal circumstances that turn a generic strategy into a targeted one.

Here's my own story to make this concrete.

I run a CPA firm. Under §199A(d)(2), that's a specified service trade or business, an SSTB. I knew that. And I realized my income was just above the QBI deduction phase-out limit for SSTBs. Which meant I would lose the ENTIRE QBI deduction. Multi-5 figures gone.

So what did I do?

- Super-maxed my year-end deductions
- Deferred receiving payment on same-year-end invoices to push income into the following year
- Chose traditional retirement contributions over backdoor Roth to lower AGI now
- Kept my W-2 salary as low as reasonable to reduce the taxable income pushing me over the phase-out

Result: I got back under the SSTB phase-out threshold and preserved the full QBI deduction. If I hadn't known I was an SSTB, I wouldn't have accelerated expenses that aggressively, and I would have paid nearly 5 figures more in tax on that alone.

That's Level 5. And it's completely different for every viewer.

SSTBs need to know they're SSTBs. E-com companies need to know how they're accounting for inventory. Construction companies need to know cash vs accrual and §460 long-term contract rules. Real estate investors need to know material participation, real estate professional status, and passive activity grouping. Doctors need to know if they qualify for the QBI deduction at all. W-2 earners at high income need to know phase-outs on their credits and deductions.

Every industry, every filing status, every income tier has rules that don't apply to anyone else. Level 5 is knowing yours.

Or, at a minimum, working with someone who is managing all of it for you.

Those are the 5 levels

Level 1: know your numbers
Level 2: fill the free buckets.
Level 3: match your entity to your income.
Level 4: manage the year, not the return.
Level 5: master your personal tax edge.

For me, doing all 5 well is the difference between paying 15% and paying closer to 25% on multi-6-figure net income. That's a $25K difference in my own tax bill. For clients at the same income level, that's the average. Much more at 7 figures.
