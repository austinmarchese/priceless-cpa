Without diving in to all the boring details, let me first briefly explain what an S-Corp actually is.

An S-Corp is a very inflexible business entity that should be used as the final barrier between you or your trust, and the business activities that you're actively involved in.

Don't plan on opening a new S-Corp for every new business idea that you have.

Don't put a bunch of your property, vehicles, or investments into your S-Corp and think it's going to save you a bunch of money in taxes.

## #1 — Paying less in Social Security and Medicare taxes

A common theme you are going to hear with all these S-Corp tax benefits is that they are directly impacted by how much salary you pay yourself.

And the savings that come from your social security and medicare taxes are no different.

The IRS requires you, as the shareholder of your S-Corp, to compensate yourself with a regular W-2 salary in return for the services that you provide to your corporation.

In short, the less salary you pay yourself, the less in social security and medicare taxes you'll pay.

What's considered reasonable is a massive gray area.

And if the IRS finds the salary you went with to be unreasonable, or if you didn't pay yourself a salary at all, they have the authority to reclassify all the money you took from the business as your salary, and demand payroll taxes as well as late penalties and interest.

So the answer to the question you've probably been wondering — "how much salary is reasonable" — is enough to avoid problems with the IRS. And realistically they're only going after the very low hanging fruit for them, which is people who made a bunch of money and didn't pay themselves any salary.

So what exactly are the savings here?

Well, for every additional dollar of salary you pay yourself, up to **$176,100**, you'll have to tack on 15.3% in taxes, plus whatever your state requires you to pay, which could be any or all of unemployment insurance, disability insurance, paid family leave, etc.

Now half of those taxes will be a business expense that will lower your income tax. So it's not a complete loss.

Additionally, you'll face an 0.9% obamacare tax if you pay yourself above $200k as a single guy or gal, or $250k if you're married.

So does that mean you should automatically pay yourself the lowest possible amount?

NO!

Sometimes paying yourself too small of a salary can lead to you paying more in taxes.

## #2 — Maximizing the qualified business income deduction

This might be the single greatest tax benefit of all time for business owners, and Trump just made it permanent with the passing of the new big beautiful bill.

Why is it so great?

Because as long as you meet some very basic hurdles, you can get a 20% deduction on all your business income, for doing nothing.

So what do you need to know?

Well, in short, you'll only lose out on the Qualified Business Income deduction if you don't have enough W-2 wages paid by your business.

If you already have a few full time employees, great, you're probably already there.

But this day and age, there's alot of solopreneurs, lean teams, and businesses that run on contractors out there.

And they are all at risk of losing out on this deduction permanently.

You see, if your income is over about 220k single 430k MFJ, that 20% deduction against your business income needs to be greater or equal to 50% of the W-2 wages your business pays.

Sounds complicated, but here's a simple rule of thumb you can follow.

Take your bottom line profit (before paying yourself) and multiply it by 2/7, and pay yourself exactly that.

Say your business's net profit before paying yourself a salary is $1 million.

You'd want to pay yourself, as a w-2 salary, exactly $285,714.29, or $1 million * 2/7.

That'd mean, after paying yourself, the business profited $714,285.71.

So you should get a 20% deduction, worth $142,857

The only way you qualify for that $142.8k deduction is if the business had double that total in W-2 wages paid ($285,714.29)

Well that happens to be exactly what you paid yourself

If you pay yourself any more than that, you are paying thousands in extra payroll taxes and are losing out on more of the qualified business income deduction (because now there is less profit to base the 20% on).

If you pay yourself any less than that, you are losing out on the remaining qualified business income deduction that you should be getting.

In order to hit the sweet spot, you need

1. timely and accurate numbers so you know exactly how much the business is making
2. a tax strategist that knows what they are doing

Being over or under the sweet spot leads to you paying thousands if not tens of thousands of dollars more in taxes and it's completely avoidable.

Let's say you aren't at risk of losing out on this deduction. In that case, it probably makes sense to pay yourself as little as you reasonably can.

The more profit that's left in the business (compared to on your W-2) will lead to a greater Qualified business income deduction and less payroll taxes.

## #3 — Maximizing your pass through entity tax election

What is the pass through entity tax election?

Well usually, S-Corps don't pay taxes.

The income passes through to your tax return as the owner, and you pay the taxes personally.

The pass through entity tax election is an optional election you can make, to shift your state income taxes, that you would have already been paying, to your business.

Then you simply get a tax credit to where you don't have to pay these taxes again on your personal tax return.

Why would you do that?

It creates an additional business expense that you can write off.

That tax is based on your business's bottom line net profit, and the amount depends on your state.

Say you net $500,000 with your S-Corp in California.

At a rate of 9.3%, you could shift **$46.5k** worth of California taxes that you'd already be paying anyways, to your business.

And that'd create a $46.5k write off, without paying any additional taxes.

This is another deduction that is heavily impacted by how much you pay yourself.

The more you pay yourself as a salary, the less profit the business will show, meaning you get to deduct less pass through taxes.

Another thing to consider, is that the more pass through entity taxes you write off, you get less of a qualified business income deduction.

All these strategies are very interrelated and can not be taken out of context.

How do you know if you should be making this election?

If your business is an S-Corporation or Partnership, and you are even somewhat profitable, chances are you should make this election.

With the passing of the most recent tax bill, you can deduct $40,000 of your personal state and local taxes on your personal tax return.

But keep in mind, you already get a standard deduction of $15,000 if your single and $30,000 if you're married.

That means you don't actually need to write your taxes off to get that standard deduction amount.

But you can double dip, and take the standard deduction on your personal tax return, while also writing off your business state taxes on your business tax return.
