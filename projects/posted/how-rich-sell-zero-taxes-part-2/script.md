That section of the tax code is Section 1202, Qualified Small Business Stock, aka QSBS.

The IRS allows you to exclude 100% of your capital gains when you sell your QSBS up to $15 million, or 10x the amount of your "basis" in your stock.

## What qualifies as QSBS?

First, the business that you own shares in must be a C-Corporation based in the US.

When you're first issued the shares, the company's assets can't be more than $75 million. This strategy is specifically for founders and investors who get in early.

Next, the business must be an active business, it can't just be a passive investment corporation. Almost all businesses will qualify here.

Additionally, you must have gotten the stock directly from the company, not second hand off a previous investor.

Lastly, the company can't be in certain professional service, like law, accounting, and consulting. It also can't be a financial services business or a farming business.

So all in all, the majority of business owners are going to qualify for this exclusion or can qualify with a little bit of intention and preparation.

## Mistakes that destroy QSBS tax savings

### Mistake 1: Messing up the holding period requirements

Once the business meets all the criteria I mentioned, the only thing you need to do is make sure you meet the holding period requirements and you are going to avoid taxes.

You need to have held the stock for 5 years in order to get the 100% exclusion, or starting with shares you get after July 4th, 2025, at least 3 years for a partial gain.

Most of the time that's not going to be an issue, but there's two big exceptions I see people mess up constantly.

**1a. Not filing an 83(b) election** — the 5 year clock never started running in the first place.

The clock only starts running when the shares are fully vested, not when they are granted to you.

However, there is an exception for tax purposes. If you file an 83b election within 30 days of being granted the shares, the IRS will treat the shares as if they fully vested and will start the holding period clock, even if the shares haven't fully vested.

**1b. The company wasn't a C-Corporation** — which also means the 5 year clock never started running.

The company must be a C-corporation during "substantially all" of your QSBS holding period. So if there is maybe a few months in the beginning of your holding period where the company was another entity you might be able to argue for the full exclusion but I wouldn't try and take too much leeway on that. The company needs to be a C-Corp.

### Mistake 2: Not evaluating the downsides of a C-Corp before committing to QSBS

Don't get me wrong, the tax savings of QSBS when it works out are unparalleled. It might be the single best section of the whole tax code.

But if you aren't actually able to pull of an exit, sometimes a C-Corporation entity structure can be costly.

That's because of two reasons.

1. C-Corporations don't get the "Qualified business income deduction" which is a straight up 20% deduction of business profit that S-Corps and LLCs get.
2. Unless you are very strategic about running a fat salary to yourself to get all the profit out of the business (which may not be possible when you have investors), C-Corp income is double taxed. Once when the business has a profit, and again when you take cash out of the business.

So you have to evaluate the pros and cons of both setups. Paying taxes twice and losing the qualified business income deduction could cost you significantly if you don't have a tax professional in your corner.

That's why the tech startup world is the main industry that benefits from QSBS.

### Mistake 3: Getting hit with 10%+ state tax rates

The third mistake QSBS founders make, is they do all this work to build a great company, finally pull off a once in a lifetime exit, but still get hit with 10%+ state tax rates.

5 states don't conform to QSBS and will tax you anyways:

California, Alabama, Mississippi, Pennsylvania, and New Jersey have no QSBS benefits.

Hawaii and Massachusetts only give you a partial exclusion.

So long story short, you can move residences to literally any other state and avoid handing over an extra 10-14% to California.

### 1. Gift shares to a trust to stack the QSBS exclusion limits

If you are going to run into the limit because you are going to have more than a $15 million gain to report, you can gift shares to a trust for your family members. You can gift up to $15 million in your lifetime without any tax consequences for you or your gift recipient. Double that if you combine it with your spouse.

So the smart play is to gift shares to a trust that benefit your kids. You can even set up multiple trusts for multiple kids/grandkids to stack the $15 million exclusion limit and avoid tens of millions of dollars in generational taxes for you and your family.

### 2. Roll over QSBS into a new company if you haven't held the stock for 5 years

Not meeting the 5 year holding period is not the end of the world. If you have an exit before 5 years, you can rollover part or all of the proceeds into a new qualified small business through a section 1045 rollover and you'll still avoid taxes on the exit. There's alot of factors to consider here, it's best to run this one by a CPA before you have your exit.

### 3. Convert your company from an LLC or S-Corp to a C-Corp

Before the company has $75 million in assets, and when it's clear you're going to have a big exit.

Just because you didn't turn your company into a C-Corp from the beginning doesn't mean you're too late to get the tax benefits.

You should evaluate the pros and cons of turning into a C-Corp with the specific goal of QSBS in mind for the founders and early investors.
